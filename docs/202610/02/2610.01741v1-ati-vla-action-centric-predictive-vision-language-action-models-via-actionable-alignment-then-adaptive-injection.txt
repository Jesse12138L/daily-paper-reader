Abstract
Predictive Vision–Language–Action (VLA) models aim to improve robotic manip-
ulation via future observation or world dynamics forecasting. However, existing
approaches often fail to realize this potential and underperform direct action pre-
diction models. We argue that these limitations stem from modality misalignment
between observations and actions, together with joint optimization conflicts that
drive learning away from an action-centric objective. To this end, we introduce
ATI-VLA, an Action-Centric Predictive Vision–Language–Action framework via
Actionable Alignment Then Adaptive Injection. Specifically, it follows a two-step
design: 1) Actionable Representation Alignment via a Shared Codebook. It aligns
predictive observation and action representations by mapping both modalities into a
shared discrete latent space via a unified codebook, making predictive observation
latents readily usable for action generation and mitigating modality misalignment.
2) Action-Centric Adaptive Injection of Predictive Latents. Building upon this, it
then injects predictive observation latents into action decoding as explicit predictive
priors via a lightweight adaptive side-path, enabling adaptive predictive guidance
under a single action-centric objective. Extensive experiments on both simulation
and real-world robotic tasks demonstrate that ATI-VLA achieves state-of-the-art
performance with faster convergence.

Introduction
Vision–Language–Action (VLA) models have emerged as a unified paradigm for robotic manipulation,
traditionally mapping observations and language instructions directly to low-level actions [18, 59, 27,
4, 32, 51, 52, 29], as shown in Fig. 1(a). More recently, predictive VLA [25, 73, 69, 67] approaches
extend this paradigm by explicitly forecasting future observations or world dynamics to provide
anticipatory guidance for action generation, as shown in Fig. 1(b).
However, our analysis reveals that existing predictive VLA approaches generally underperform non-
predictive VLA baselines, as illustrated in Fig. 1(d). This indicates that introducing future prediction
as an auxiliary task does not reliably translate into effective improvements in action generation.
We argue that this performance gap arises because these predictive approaches fail to maintain an

Observation and Action. As shown in Fig. 1(e), latent distributions analysis of recent predictive
VLA models [67, 45] reveals a pronounced modal gap between observation prediction and action
generation. This gap weakens the grounding of predictive representations in action generation, thereby
diluting the action-centric learning focus. ii) Optimization Conflicts Induced by Joint Training.
Existing predictive VLA paradigms typically introduce future prediction as an auxiliary task by
directly adding its loss to the action objective. As illustrated in Fig. 1(f), such joint optimization leads
to unstable training dynamics and slow convergence. Moreover, competing gradients from prediction
and action objectives can divert optimization away from action-centric objectives and, in extreme
cases, result in training collapse [67]. Together, these factors hinder predictive VLA approaches
from fully realizing their performance potential. We provide a more in-depth analysis of these two
issues in Appendix C.2.
To address these challenges, we propose ATI-VLA, an action-centric predictive VLA framework
via Actionable Alignment Then Adaptive Injection, explicitly aligning predictive representations
with action generation and injecting predictive information under a single action-centric objective.
Specifically, as shown in Fig. 1(c), ATI-VLA follows a two-step design: 1) Actionable Representation
Alignment via a Shared Codebook. To bridge the modal gap between predictive observations and
action generation, ATI-VLA introduces an actionable representation alignment via a shared codebook.
By mapping both modalities into a unified discrete latent space, predictive representations are directly
grounded in the action space, making them readily compatible with the action decoding process
rather than being implicitly entangled through joint training as in prior approaches. 2) Action-Centric
Adaptive Injection of Predictive Latents. Building upon the aligned and actionable representations,
ATI-VLA further introduces an action-centric adaptive injection mechanism to integrate predictive
information into action generation. To enable predictive guidance to adapt as action generation
progresses, we employ a lightweight adaptive side-path that modulates the action decoding process
conditioned on predictive latents. Moreover, to avoid predictive information from overly interfering
with action decoding, this modulation is applied at regular layer intervals, allowing predictive
guidance while preserving fine-grained control. Crucially, this injection is optimized solely with
the action loss, ensuring that predictive information serves purely as guidance without inducing
optimization conflicts.
Extensive experimental results demonstrate that, by addressing the aforementioned challenges, ATI-
VLA is able to fully unlock the potential of predictive VLA paradigms. Across both simulation
benchmarks and real-world robotic tasks, ATI-VLA consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance,
while exhibiting faster convergence compared to existing predictive approaches. To summarize, our
main contributions are as follows:

Problem Definition. As discussed above, this design often introduces a modality gap between

predictive representations to enhance action generation while preserving action-centric optimization.

2.2.2
Actionable Representation Alignment
As discussed above, we first bridge the observation–action modality gap in predictive VLA via an
explicit alignment step. Recent works [10, 5] have explored extracting action semantics from con-
secutive frames into compact latent representations, motivating structured cross-modal abstractions.
Inspired by these efforts, we propose Actionable Representation Alignment via a Shared Codebook to
bridge this modality gap, as illustrated in Fig. 2. To ensure effective alignment, we enforce temporal
consistency and causal alignment between observation and action representations by adopting a
symmetric, predictive design across both branches. Predictive and action representations are mapped
into a shared discrete latent via a unified codebook, grounding predictive latents in the action space.

the future pair (Ot+n, At+n), where n denotes the action chunk size, we encode Ot and At using

The predicted observation frames in Fig. 5a, generated from aligned action representations, exhibit

precise placement, demonstrating the practical applicability of ATI-VLA. Additional qualitative
results are in Appendix C.3, with real-world videos in the supplementary material.

rates than ATI-VLA w/o alignment, especially on long-horizon tasks. Compared with prior general
and predictive VLA baselines, ATI-VLA also exhibits faster optimization and better final performance
(Fig. 1(f)). These results suggest that actionable representation alignment provides stable predictive
guidance and improves training efficiency.

Related Work
Vision–Language–Action Models. Vision–Language Models (VLMs) [1, 24, 55, 15, 12, 39, 54, 61,
43, 48, 57, 56, 72, 62, 63, 68, 42, 40] have substantially advanced robotic manipulation by tightly
coupling visual perception with linguistic reasoning [53, 28, 66, 26, 41]. Building on this foundation,
RT-2 [4] pioneered the integration of pre-trained VLMs into Vision–Language–Action modeling,
9


Figure 6: Convergence Comparison. ATI-VLA converges faster and performs better than ATI-VLA
w/o alignment, while Fig. 1(f) further shows faster convergence over prior predictive VLA baselines.
while RT-X [46] further scaled this paradigm with large-scale data to improve generalization across
tasks. A series of subsequent approaches, including OpenVLA [21], OpenVLA-OFT [20], and the
π-series [2, 18] models, further adopt and extend this paradigm, demonstrating strong performance
across a wide range of robotic manipulation tasks.
Predictive VLA Models. Recently, increasing attention has been paid to incorporating future
prediction into Vision–Language–Action (VLA) models to enhance robotic manipulation [69, 67,
30, 65, 34, 17]. Some methods [14, 3] employ auxiliary image or video generation models to
predict future frames and condition action prediction on the generated goals, introducing additional
inference latency. Accordingly, subsequent methods aim to unify action prediction with future
modeling, ranging from pixel-level forecasting [35, 71] to world knowledge prediction, within a
single framework. Despite their strengths, these methods often suffer from modality misalignment
and joint optimization conflicts that weaken action-centric learning. In contrast, ATI-VLA explicitly
aligns predictive latents with actions and injects them under a single action-centric objective.
Robotic Manipulation in Learned Latent Spaces. Recent research explores compact, structured
latent representations for robotic planning and control [58, 37, 22, 23]. VQ-BeT [22] and STAR [23]
learn discrete action or skill representations to support transformer-based policy learning, while other
approaches learn transferable latent skills from internet-scale human videos [60, 10, 5, 9, 38]. Both
lines of work emphasize learning latent action or skill representations, whereas ATI-VLA targets the
observation–action modality gap in predictive VLA. It also shares the use of discrete codebooks with
recent action tokenizers [47, 13], but differs in the supervision and role of its codebook. Specifically, it
learns shared, action-grounded prototypes by jointly predicting future observations and actions rather
than reconstructing inputs. The resulting observation latents guide action decoding under a single
action objective rather than serve as output action tokens. More closely related, VITA [36] also learns
a shared visual–action codebook. ATI-VLA nevertheless differs in: (1) codebook architecture and
learning, using a unified decoder for joint future observation–action prediction rather than separate
autoencoders for modality-specific reconstruction; (2) policy architecture, injecting predictive latents
through an adaptive side-path rather than generating shared tokens for dual visual–action decoding;
and (3) optimization, freezing the alignment module and using only the action loss in Stage 2, whereas
VITA [36] jointly optimizes observation and action losses during co-training.
5
Conclusion
We propose ATI-VLA, an action-centric predictive VLA framework that overcomes modality mis-
alignment and optimization conflicts via an Actionable Alignment Then Adaptive Injection design. It
first aligns observations and actions in a shared discrete latent space, then adaptively injects predictive
latents under a single action objective, achieving state-of-the-art performance with faster convergence.
This demonstrates how explicit alignment and adaptive injection can fully realize the potential of
predictive VLA for robust robotic manipulation.

A
Implementation Details.
A.1
Model Details
Structural Details of Actionable Representation Alignment. In the Actionable Representation
Alignment stage of ATI-VLA, we implement three core components:
• Observation Encoder employs a patch-embedding layer followed by a spatial Transformer
to extract visual tokens, which are then quantized into a shared discrete latent space via a
unified codebook. It processes a single input frame and outputs a set of discrete latent codes
that capture spatial and semantic information of the scene.
• Action Encoder uses a lightweight MLP to project the current action vector into a
spatially-aligned embedding grid, which is similarly quantized through the same shared
codebook. This ensures that action representations share the same discrete latent structure
as visual observations, enabling cross-modal alignment.
• Unified Decoder is built with stacked self-attention and cross-attention layers, taking
the quantized latent representations of both observation and action as context to jointly
predict the future observation and future action. The self-attention captures intra-modal
dependencies, while cross-attention fuses the aligned observation and action latents for
coherent multi-modal prediction.
Base Framework and Implementation. ATI-VLA adopts OpenVLA-OFT [20] as its implementa-
tion framework, with OpenVLA-7B [21] serving as the underlying backbone. We retain the standard
OpenVLA-OFT implementation setup and incorporate the proposed actionable representation align-
ment and adaptive latent injection mechanisms.
Inference Mechanism. Unlike video-generation-based policies, ATI-VLA does not require explicit
future-observation decoding during inference. Consistent with the use of predictive latent representa-
tions in DreamVLA [67] and Seer [45], ATI-VLA directly exploits predictive information in latent
space to guide action generation. Specifically, in stage 1, the alignment module is trained with both
future-observation and action supervision to learn future-aware, action-grounded representations.
This module is subsequently frozen but retained at inference time: the current observation is mapped
into the learned latent space, and the resulting predictive latent is adaptively injected into the action
decoder as an explicit guidance signal. Therefore, although ATI-VLA does not perform an explicit
future-observation rollout at test time, the predictive information learned in Stage 1 continues to
directly guide action generation through the retained adaptive side-path.

A.2
Training Details
Simulation Training Setup. For the LIBERO benchmark, the network is adapted using Low-Rank
Adaptation (LoRA), configured with a rank of 32 and a scaling parameter α = 64. Training proceeds
with a batch size of 64, initiated at a learning rate of 5 × 10−4. The action sequence length is set
to n = 8. Validation is performed on benchmark tasks at intervals of every 5,000 steps, with the
model checkpoint delivering the highest validation performance being selected for final reporting.
Regarding the RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark, we utilize a batch size of 32, an initial learning rate of
5 × 10−4, and an action chunk size of 25.
Real-World Training Setup. For real-world bimanual manipulation, we follow the standard
OpenVLA-OFT setup, using one main-camera view and two wrist-camera views with an action
chunk length of n = 25. The model is fine-tuned with LoRA, maintaining a rank of 32 and α = 64.
The training regimen spans 80,000 steps with a batch size of 32. The learning rate is initially set to
5×10−4 and is subsequently decayed to 5×10−5 after 50,000 steps. Starting from the 60,000th step,
model checkpoints are evaluated every 5,000 steps. The checkpoint exhibiting the best performance
is retained as the final model for deployment.
B
Benchmarks and Baselines
B.1
Simulation Benchmark
LIBERO is a language-conditioned manipulation benchmark that evaluates complementary facets of
robotic capability through multiple task suites.
• LIBERO-Spatial contains 10 tasks with identical objects arranged in varied layouts, empha-
sizing spatial reasoning.
• LIBERO-Object includes 10 tasks with fixed layouts but different object instances, testing
object-level generalization.
• LIBERO-Goal comprises 10 tasks sharing object categories and layouts while varying goals,
assessing goal-conditioned behavior diversity.
• LIBERO-Long consists of 10 challenging long-horizon tasks across diverse objects and
scenes, stressing temporal reasoning and sequential control.
We follow the standard protocol and use 50 expert demonstrations per task from the official release.
RoboTwin 2.0 provides a large-scale benchmark for bimanual manipulation, built on an asset library
of 731 objects across 147 categories. It defines 50 dual-arm tasks with expert trajectories generated via
an MLLM-driven simulation pipeline. To enhance robustness and sim-to-real transfer, it incorporates
structured domain randomization across clutter, lighting, backgrounds, table height, and instructions.
For evaluation, four tasks are selected: Click Bell, Open Laptop, Place Container Plate, and Place
Bread Skillet, each evaluated over 100 trials.
B.2
Real-World Tasks
Task Descriptions. We deploy ATI-VLA on Galaxea R1 Lite and AgileX Cobot Magic platforms
for real-world evaluation across five long-horizon sequential manipulation tasks that span four core
capability categories: Object Packing (evaluating dual-arm coordination and container manipulation),
Markers Collection (testing fine-grained precision manipulation), Plate Handover (assessing dynamic
object transfer and bimanual interaction), and T-shirt Folding (examining deformable object handling
in both standard and OOD configurations). These tasks collectively challenge fundamental robotic
capabilities including precision manipulation, bimanual coordination, bimanual handover, and dy-
namic adaptation under uncertainty. Tasks requiring dual-arm coordination (Object Packing, Plate
Handover, and both T-shirt Folding variants) evaluate spatial-temporal synchronization and inter-arm
transfer, while Markers Collection isolates fine motor control in a single-arm precision scenario. Each
task is evaluated through progressive stage success rates to assess intermediate performance, with
50 human-teleoperated demonstrations collected per task to ensure sufficient training data diversity.
Details are provided below.

• Object Packing
Instruction:“Pack the object on the table into the bag.”
This dual-arm sequential manipulation task evaluates coordinated bimanual operation for

Instruction:“Fold the T-shirt.”

Instruction:“Fold the T-shirt.”

Augmentation strategies include randomized object positions, container orientations, and initial arm

Modality Gaps.

(2) When the first dominant failure occurs. As shown in Table 16, the first dominant failures

(3) Whether alignment is lower immediately before failure. As shown in Table 17, using cross-

at key stages, including wrong target selection, incorrect approach or placement poses, and subgoal-
switching errors. Such failures are especially frequent in multi-stage transition states, where predictive
semantics must be converted into a new action regime. ATI-VLA effectively mitigates these failures
by maintaining stronger action-grounded predictive representations.

C.2.3
Why Is the Modality Gap Severe in Predictive VLA Settings.
Predictive VLA makes the observation–action modality gap more severe because the predicted
representation is often learned under an observation-oriented objective but later used for action
generation. Specifically, future-observation prediction encourages visually accurate reconstruction of
the next scene. However, many visual details, such as background texture, illumination, or pixel-level
appearance, are not directly relevant to control. As a result, the predictive latent may encode rich
visual information while failing to emphasize action-relevant factors such as target identity, contact
state, object pose, or subgoal transition. When such observation-oriented latents are used to guide
the action decoder, the policy must implicitly translate them into executable action chunks, which
enlarges the modality gap.
This issue is further amplified by joint prediction-action optimization. In conventional predictive
VLA training, the shared representation is optimized by both the prediction loss and the action loss.
These two objectives can prefer different features: the prediction loss favors visually faithful future
states, whereas the action loss favors compact and control-relevant abstractions. As analyzed in
our gradient-conflict study, their gradients can point in conflicting directions, pulling the shared
representation away from a purely action-centric solution. Therefore, the modality gap in predictive
VLA is not only caused by the inherent difference between observations and actions, but also by the
mismatch between the prediction objective and the downstream control objective.
ATI-VLA addresses this issue through the align-then-inject design. Stage 1 uses both observation and
action information to learn an action-grounded shared latent space, so that predictive latents encode
future-oriented but control-relevant semantics. Stage 2 then injects these aligned latents into action
decoding under a single action objective, avoiding further prediction-action optimization conflict.
This makes predictive information more directly usable for control rather than leaving the policy to
bridge the modality gap implicitly.

D.2
Interval Injection
We analyze interval injection as a local first-order perturbation of the action-token decoding trajectory.
This local view is important because standard dot-product self-attention is not globally Lipschitz on
an unbounded input domain [19]. Instead of assuming global Lipschitzness of the full Transformer,
we only require bounded first-order sensitivity in the neighborhood actually traversed by the clean and

E
Limitations and Future Work
Although ATI-VLA shows strong performance on simulation benchmarks and real-world robotic
tasks, the current training and evaluation are still based on a relatively limited set of manipulation
datasets and task scenarios. In particular, the real-world experiments mainly rely on task-specific
teleoperated demonstrations, which validate the effectiveness of our align-then-inject design but
do not fully cover the diversity of objects, scenes, embodiments, and interaction patterns in open-
world manipulation. A natural direction for future work is to scale ATI-VLA to larger and more
heterogeneous robot datasets. Such data could help the alignment module learn a more comprehensive
action-grounded latent space and improve the transferability of predictive latents across tasks and
platforms. Future work may also incorporate human videos, cross-embodiment trajectories, and
self-supervised interaction data to provide richer priors about object affordances and action-induced
state transitions, further extending ATI-VLA toward open-ended long-horizon manipulation.
F
Broader Impact and Potential Risks
ATI-VLA aims to improve the reliability and efficiency of VLA models for long-horizon robotic ma-
nipulation. By grounding predictive representations in action generation, it may benefit applications
such as assistive robotics, warehouse automation, and flexible manufacturing, while also offering a
general direction for action-centric predictive learning in embodied AI.
Potential risks may arise when stronger robotic policies are deployed in unconstrained or safety-
critical environments, where prediction errors, distribution shifts, or incorrect instruction grounding
could lead to unintended behaviors. Practical deployment should therefore include safety constraints,
human oversight, permission control, and thorough real-world validation. Future work should further
study uncertainty-aware execution and failure detection for safer open-world operation.
