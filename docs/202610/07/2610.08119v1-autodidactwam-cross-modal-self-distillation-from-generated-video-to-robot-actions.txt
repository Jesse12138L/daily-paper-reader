# AutodidactWAM: Cross-Modal Self-Distillation from Generated Video to Robot Actions 

Sergei Kurchev 1,2, Iaroslav Kolomiets 1,2, Miguel Altamirano Cabrera 1,2, Artem Lykov 2,1, and Dzmitry Tsetserukou 1

Abstract — World-action models (WAMs) such as Cosmos 3 jointly generate future video and robot actions from an observation and instruction. Adapting one such model, with a lightweight LoRA fine-tune, to a robot absent from pre-training, a Unitree G1 humanoid with five-fingered BrainCo hands, exposes a video–action asymmetry: the video already renders plausible task executions, while the co-generated action is systematically mis-targeted. We score closed-loop real-robot trials at three cumulative stages: pre-grasp (hand reaching a graspable pose), grasp, and pick-and-place, where the native ac-tion, on pilot objects, succeeds only ≈ 17% /10% /7% of the time, worse still on held-out objects. We propose AutodidactWAM : a hand-pose estimator trained without teleoperation, followed by inverse kinematics, run on the model’s own generated video to recover an action estimate ˆa through those same three stages. Paired with the native prediction a−, the resulting preferred target a+ fine-tunes only the action-related layers, with the training loss evaluated on action outputs while the generated video is teacher-forced. We compare two ways to use this pair: plain supervised relabeling and a rectified-flow adaptation of Diffusion-DPO, rather than committing to either. After one-time embodiment adaptation, self-distillation requires no addi-tional task-specific teleoperation. The recovered-action ˆa gate reaches ≈ 75% /47% /42% pre-grasp/grasp/pick-and-place suc-cess, against the native 17% /10% /7% . A hybrid self-distillation objective combining preference supervision, supervised target fitting, and Cartesian trajectory anchoring (DPO+SFT+DTW) produces the strongest policy among the configurations tested: on Oreo, our training object, it reaches 90% pre-grasp and 

20% full-task success; on the held-out object, it reaches 80% 

and 30% . Plain Flow-DPO, trained without any additional losses, reaches 0% success everywhere despite 1.000 validation preference accuracy—evidence that it is the combination of terms, not the contrastive objective alone, that drives the gains above. 

I. INTRODUCTION Progress on generalist robot manipulation is gated by action-labeled data: teleoperation is slow, robot-specific, and must be repeated for every new embodiment [1]–[3], hardest for dexterous multi-fingered hands. World-action models 

(WAMs) address this via large-scale pre-training that predicts both future video and actions from vision and language [4]– [8]. Cosmos 3 [5] is one such model: video and action tokens are noised and denoised jointly within one rectified-flow sampling trajectory [9], [10] (Sec. III-A).

We adapt Cosmos 3 to a Unitree G1 humanoid with five-fingered BrainCo hands, an embodiment absent from pre-training, via a lightweight LoRA fine-tune on a mixed in-house G1 corpus. The two output modalities transfer very unequally: the generated video is usually visually plausible and temporally coherent, although occasional hallucinations remain and are characterized separately (Sec. IV); the co-generated action output looks plausible on its own but is systematically mis-targeted in space, completing the task in arXiv:2610.08119v1 [cs.RO] 6 Oct 2026 only ≈ 7% of closed-loop pick-and-place trials and reaching pre-grasp in just ≈ 17% (Table I). This suggests task-consistent information is represented more effectively in the generated video than in the co-generated action tokens, and more fine-tuning does not fix it: SFT loss decreases and DPO validation preference accuracy saturates at 1.000 within the first round, while the on-policy task metric that tracks real behavior — headline wrist DTW/path — does not follow (Fig. 3, Sec. VII). This points to a residual cross-modal consistency problem between the video and action outputs of one generation, related to a forward–inverse asymmetry [11], an executability gap [12], or a mismatch between plausible video and control-relevant dynamics [13] in concurrent work. We recover that correspondence with a frozen perception stack — hand detection [14], a hand-pose model [15] adapted once to the robot hand, and inverse kinematics — run on each generated rollout to give a full action trajectory ˆa, paired with the native prediction a− to fine-tune the action-related layers toward the resulting target a+ (Sec. III-G). We compare two ways to use this pair (Sec. III-D): plain supervised relabeling onto a+, and a rectified-flow adaptation of Diffusion-DPO [16] that prefers a+ over a−,since token-level DPO [17] needs a tractable likelihood actions do not have. The generated video is teacher-forced as clean conditioning in both cases, so gradients update only the action-related layers and cannot degrade video quality; using the model’s own prediction as the rejected sample mirrors self-play preference optimization [18]. Every training example needs only a composited anchor frame; seeds and LLM-generated prompt variations [19] turn it into an effectively unbounded stream of (video , ˆa, a −) tuples with no manual annotation, in the lineage of self-training and self-distillation [18], [20], [21] applied here across the outputs of one model. The perception stack itself carries only a one-time training cost from synthetic renders (Sec. III-C), so the method adds no further teleoperation as it scales; relative to recent work on the same gap, we repair the action output offline rather than with reinforcement learning [11]–[13], [22] (Sec. II).

VLA policies and video-as-control. Action policies trained on heterogeneous robot corpora [3], [6], [23], [24], often with a diffusion action head [25], still generally need demonstrations on a robot outside the training mixture; our starting point — Cosmos 3 with a lightweight LoRA on the new embodiment — sits in this family, and our contribution begins where it usually ends, when the adapted action branch still does not produce competent behavior. Separately, world models have served as planners or manipulation pretraining, with a separate policy or inverse-dynamics model turning predicted frames into actions [7], [8], [26]–[30]. We differ in that video and action are two outputs of one model and the generated video repairs that same model’s action branch rather than driving a downstream controller; the recovered target a+ is used contrastively against the model’s own a−,not as a plain regression target. 

The video–action gap, and preference optimization. 

Several recent methods target the same mismatch: WAV [11] frames it as a forward–inverse asymmetry with a subgoal generator and a sparse inverse model; EVA [12] names it the executability gap and RL-tunes the video branch against an inverse-dynamics reward trained on real robot data; VAMPO [13] and WMPO [22] apply policy optimization to sharpen visual dynamics or run in-world-model RL for a VLA policy. We differ on four points: we correct the 

action branch and leave video untouched; the optimization is offline and preference-based, with no reward model, no training-time rollouts, and no real-robot inverse-dynamics data; the extractor is a hand-pose reconstructor with IK, for the dexterous contact-rich regime where low-dimensional inverse models are weakest; and every signal is a contrastive pair against the model’s own a− for the same generation. That contrastive step builds on DPO [17] and its diffusion extension [16] (Sec. III-D adapts the latter to rectified flow) and on self-improvement from a model’s own outputs [18], [20], [21]; AutodidactWAM applies this across modalities 

of one model, the stronger (video) supervising the weaker (actions). III. METHOD AutodidactWAM has three stages (Fig. 2). First, from one anchor frame and a language instruction the world model generates a video rollout together with its native action prediction. Second, a frozen perception stack reads the 

generated video and recovers a second, independent action trajectory. Third, the model’s action branch is fine-tuned to prefer the recovered trajectory over its own native one, with a preference objective adapted to the model’s rectified-flow formulation and with the generated video teacher-forced so that only the action tokens are updated. These stages are then iterated : because the pair describes the action branch as it currently behaves, it is regenerated from the updated model at a fixed step interval (Sec. III-B). The remainder of this section makes each stage precise: Sec. III-A recaps the model, Sec. III-B constructs the contrastive pair and its on-policy refresh, Sec. III-C details the extractor that recovers ˆa, Sec. III-D gives the objectives, Sec. III-E explains how the generated video is teacher-forced, Sec. III-F states where the generated video comes from, and Sec. III-G lists implementation details. 

A. Preliminaries: Cosmos 3 as a joint video–action flow model 

Cosmos 3 [5] is trained as a rectified flow [9], [10]: for clean latent z, noise n ∼ N (0 , I ), t ∈ [0 , 1] , the interpolant 

zt = (1 − t)z + tn and the network vθ regresses the constant velocity n − z. One network denoises video and action latent tokens jointly under full attention; they are not two separate branches or subnetworks, but two token types produced by the same generator, distinguished only by their own encoder/decoder and modality embedding (Sec. III-G lists exactly which action-specific tensors, plus a LoRA adapter, we fine-tune — every layer shared with video stays frozen). For brevity, the rest of the paper still calls this action-specific slice the “action branch,” but it names a set of I/O tensors within one generator, not a separate network. At inference in policy mode, the model is conditioned on an anchor frame I0 and prompt c (no timestep embedding on conditioning tokens) and denoises video and action tokens 

together along one sampler trajectory, emitting a rollout V

and an action chunk a ∈ RT ×57 , T ≈ 32 , for the G1’s 57 -D action space (per-arm wrist pose, fingertip targets, a fixed head term), mapped by a fixed IK step to the robot’s 26 joint commands.

ˆa = Φ( V ) — a reconstruction, not ground truth. The preferred target a+ is built from ˆa by a per-row choice (Sec. The pair is meaningful because V is visually verifiable and, empirically, correct (optionally screened [31]) while 

a− is plausible-looking but mis-targeted, so preferring a+

needs no human annotation, mirroring self-play preference optimization [18]. Because a+ is built from an estimate, we gate on ˆa directly in closed loop before any distillation (Sec. V-C; Sec. VIII discusses residual extractor bias); ˆa is resampled onto a−’s time grid, both in normalized action units. 

b) On-policy refresh: The pair is not fixed for the whole run: distillation proceeds in rounds of K optimizer steps, and at the start of each round both members are regenerated from the current checkpoint, keeping a− on-policy and a+ tied to video the current model actually produces. The reference model θref (Sec. III-D) is, for all reported results, an EMA of the policy (decay 0.999 , persisted across rounds); a fully frozen reference is an untested alternative. 

C. Recovering ˆa: the RoboHaMeR extractor 

Φ turns a generated RGB rollout into a joint-space tra-jectory through three frozen, non-contributed components, described only enough to characterize the noise floor they impose on ˆa (Sec. V-C): (i) a YOLO hand detector [14], [32] (Grounding-DINO [33] fallback) gives one box per hand per frame; (ii) RoboHaMeR, our adaptation of HaMeR [15] (its ViT-H/16 [34] encoder kept unchanged, with new heads replacing MANO’s [35]), regresses six actuator strokes, wrist rotation (6-D [36]), and metric palm depth, trained without teleoperation on ∼30 k synthetic renders of the robot swept through diverse arm/hand poses with exact, render-derived joint labels; and (iii) a differential arm IK step [37] drives the wrist onto the predicted pose, fingers seeded from the strokes, gaps interpolated, and the trajectory EMA-filtered into the 57 -D action space. On a held-out physical scene the actuator-stroke MAE is ≈0.9% and the 3-D keypoint MAE 

≈0.7 mm, bounding Φ’s precision rather than its cross-scene generalization (Sec. VIII).

E. Teacher-forcing the video: updating the action branch only 

Video and action tokens are denoised in the same pass, so a naive preference step that re-noises and re-denoises the video would route preference gradients into the video branch — exactly the component we must not disturb. We instead teacher-force the generated video: the decoded rollout V ,re-encoded to latent tokens, enters κ as clean conditioning (no timestep embedding), reusing the model’s existing idle-frame conditioning path, only now the entire video chunk is conditioning rather than a single frame. ∆θ is taken over action tokens alone; the video branch receives no gradient and the update is confined to the action encoder/decoder and the LoRA adapters. Each training step is then four action-only forward passes (policy and reference, on a+ and a−), far cheaper than a full joint denoising rollout. 

F. Where the generated video comes from 

Every result in this submission generates V with the embodiment-adapted model as it stands after its LoRA fine-tune on a mixed in-house G1 teleoperation corpus (zero-shot, ZS) — one LoRA that trains the video and action branches jointly, after which the video branch transfers well and the action branch does not; a− is that model’s native action trace, with no task- or scene-specific tuning added on top.

(I0, c, seed) → (V, a −, ˆa), none requiring teleoperation or annotation. An anchor frame is built by compositing, not filming: about ten staged shots of the G1’s hands over an empty table and ten photographs of the Oreo package are combined freely, the object placed at arbitrary reachable positions in software, so a dozen real captures yield an unbounded pool of anchors. Each anchor, with a structured action prompt [5], is sent to the model in policy mode: one forward pass returns V and the native a− (Sec. III-B); Φ

reads V and returns ˆa, cached in a manifest the pair dataset is read directly from. This supply is unbounded because three axes multiply: 

seeds (reproducible per (frame, prompt)), prompts (an LLM paraphrases the templated action prompt while keeping it valid [19], [38]), and placements (each hand pose pairs with the object anywhere reachable). The marginal cost of one more pair is one model pass plus one Φ pass — no robot time, no human labeling; a scaling sweep is future work (Sec. VIII). The starting policy is already adapted to the G1 using a mixed teleoperation corpus (Sec. V); self-distillation does not remove this initial requirement, but generates all further task-specific supervision without new teleoperated trajectories. Not every generation is usable (hallucinated hand, dropped object, frozen gripper); two cheap safeguards catch this be-fore training: anchor-pose divergence in the opening frames, and an optional vision–language plausibility judge [31], whose value we test by correlating its score with downstream 

ˆa error. V. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

A. Task, embodiment, and checkpoints 

All experiments use a single tabletop pick-and-place task — moving an Oreo package onto a tray — on a Unitree G1 humanoid with five-fingered BrainCo hands, right arm/hand only. The base model is Cosmos 3 Nano. The start checkpoint for all distillation runs, and row A, is this model adapted to the embodiment by a LoRA fine-tune (≈ 90% Oreo pick-and-place, ≈ 10% public datasets with other objects). Its native action output defines the first-round 

a−, regenerated from the current checkpoint each later round (Sec. III-B); that it already completes only ≈ 17% of pre-grasp attempts and 0% full pick-and-place on Oreo (Table I) is part of what motivates the method. The perception stack itself uses no teleoperation (Sec. III-C). 

B. Metrics and protocol 

The primary metric is closed-loop task success rate on the real robot , since offline proxies have repeatedly diverged from real behavior on this project. We score every rollout at three cumulative stages: pre-grasp , grasp (implies pre-grasp), and pick-and-place (implies both) — separating targeting, contact, and placement failures a single success bit would conflate. Two offline proxies fill the gap between closed-loop evaluations: a windowed RMSE against the demonstrated trajectory, and the RMS gap between the absolute wrist-position trajectories of a− and a+, which catches end-effector drift a per-frame residual hides. Fig. 3 tracks this headline wrist DTW/path over training (Sec. VII). Every row is evaluated on the same held-out set, disjoint from training data, over N = 20 real-robot trials per (object, condition), replayed as ≈ 4 consecutive T ≈ 32 -step chunks executed open-loop to completion between fresh camera captures ( ≈ 104 –137 s regeneration latency per chunk). The video–action asymmetry reuses this protocol’s closed-loop success for the action side (Sec. V-C); a matching video-side score has not been run (Sec. VIII), so Fig. 1’s asymmetry is argued via Table I. Data-engine generation averages ≈

8.8 s/episode for sampling and ≈ 39 s/episode for extraction. 

C. Validating the recovered actions ( ˆa gate) 

Distillation is only sensible if ˆa is actually better than 

a−, and since self-distillation never has a matched ground-truth trajectory to compare against, we validate this directly in closed loop rather than by an offline error decompo-sition. The gate check replays ˆa open-loop on the real robot (Video →Extractor →IK, no distillation) under the same three-stage protocol (Sec. V-B); Φ’s own precision on a held-out physical scene with real ground truth (Sec. III-C) separately bounds its noise floor. In the pilot, actions recovered from zero-shot generations reach ≈75% real-robot pre-grasp success against ≈17% for a− (Table I), clearing the gate.

Scope of this submission. The five conditions of Table I are the complete real-robot pilot reported in this paper; we did not have time to additionally run the further objectives and video sources of Sec. V-D. These are listed as future work (Sec. VIII), not as results in progress. VII. A NALYSIS 

An interesting side finding: plain DPO’s preference accuracy is a poor proxy here. In the completed plain-DPO run, validation preference accuracy reaches 1.000 within the first round and holds for eight rounds, while headline task-wrist DTW/path on the same checkpoints shows no trend, ranging 25 –75 mm around the unchanged base. Wrist trans-lation is represented as per-frame deltas, so a small, sign-correlated per-step error the flow-matching margin (Eq. (1)) cannot see integrates into large absolute drift; a checkpoint can satisfy the pairwise preference almost perfectly while still drifting in Cartesian space. This is consistent with plain DPO’s 0% closed-loop success (Sec. VI) and with why the Cartesian/DTW anchor term was motivated in the first place, though we do not treat this single run as conclusive on its own.

C ONCLUSION 

Adapting Cosmos 3 to a five-fingered humanoid absent from pre-training exposes a video–action asymmetry: the video branch of a jointly-trained world-action model renders the task convincingly while the native action branch of that same generation reaches pre-grasp only ≈ 17% of the time and completes the full task in ≈ 7% of closed-loop trials. Au-todidactWAM closes part of this gap by reading the model’s own generated video back through a frozen, teleoperation-free perception stack, and using the resulting estimate to fine-tune only the action branch, with no additional task-specific teleoperation collected. On the real robot, a hybrid self-distillation objective combining preference supervision, supervised target fitting, and Cartesian trajectory anchoring (DPO+SFT+DTW) produces the strongest policy among the configurations we trained, raising pre-grasp success to 90% 

on the object used to build every training pair and, without ever seeing it during distillation, to 80% on a held-out object; full pick-and-place reaches 20% and 30% on the two objects, respectively, against the untrained baseline’s 0% and 10% .A plain contrastive objective with neither the supervised nor the Cartesian-anchor term, by contrast, reaches 0% closed-loop success despite saturating its own validation preference metric — evidence that it is specifically the combination of terms, not contrastive (DPO) training alone, that makes self-distillation work here, and a caution against trusting offline preference accuracy as a stand-in for closed-loop behavior in this setting.
