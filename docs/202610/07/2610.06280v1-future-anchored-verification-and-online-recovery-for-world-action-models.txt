ABSTRACT
World action models (WAMs) have emerged as a promising paradigm for robotic
manipulation. They act by first predicting how a task should be performed and
then decoding the actions from that future. However, the remaining actions are
invalid once execution drifts from the prediction. Simply replanning from the al-
ready out of distribution state rarely restores what the task still requires; existing
execution monitors decide when to stop, but not what to restore. We observe that
the answer is already in hand: the future the WAM predicted before acting de-
picts exactly the states it intended to pass through. We introduce FAVOR (Future
Anchored Verification and Online Recovery), a lightweight framework that keeps
these predicted frames as anchors and uses them for verification and recovery. An
Anchor Verifier compares each observation with its anchor, together with the ex-
ecuted actions, to flag deviations that break the task. Anchor-Guided Recovery
uses a vision-language model to turn the flagged anchor into a short corrective
instruction. Under strengthened instruction guidance, the WAM executes this in-
struction to return to the intended future. It then resumes the task. FAVOR raises
the task success of the base WAM from 97.85% to 98.10% on LIBERO and from
72.60% to 72.98% on LIBERO-Plus without modifying the policy.

INTRODUCTION
World action models (WAMs) have emerged as a promising paradigm for robotic manipulation.
They work in a forward and backward way: they first predict the visual future a task should pass
through and then decode the actions that realize the future [12, 27, 32, 62, 69, 74, 75, 78, 82, 86, 87].
Unlike policies that map current observations and task instruction directly to actions [4, 8, 26], every
action is thus grounded in an explicit future of where the scene should go.
That future is only a plan. Imprecise actions or external disturbances can push the scene off the
predicted path, invalidating the remaining actions [33, 64]. Simply replanning from the deviated
state, however, offers little help: WAMs are trained on successful demonstrations [29, 54, 55], so
neither the future predicted from such an unfamiliar state nor the actions decoded from it can be
trusted. Execution monitors for WAMs help by flagging when the remaining actions should no
longer be trusted [1, 43, 64, 72, 79], but say nothing about what to restore. Recovery methods
address this gap, but obtain their target from outside the policy [11, 50]. This raises a natural
question: can a WAM tell when execution has left its plan, and find its way back on its own?
Our observation is that the target of recovery is already in the policy’s hands. The future the WAM
predicted depicts the states it intended to pass through, and the actions were generated under its
guidance, so the actions remain valid exactly as long as the scene follows it. When a deviation
occurs, this prediction still shows the condition that was lost, such as a mug held in the gripper,
and that state lies in the region where the policy was trained. A deviation therefore invalidates the
remaining actions, but not the states they were meant to establish. This suggests a simple principle:
use the policy’s own predicted future as an anchor.

Following this principle, we propose FAVOR (Future Anchored Verification and Online Recovery),
which keeps the predicted frames as anchors and uses them both to verify execution and to guide
recovery. Two challenges stand in the way. (1) Not every mismatch is a deviation: the anchor is an
image produced by a generative model and may contain visual artifacts, so pixel-level differences
do not necessarily imply a semantic mismatch. (2) The challenge is to convey the anchor to the
policy and elicit the intended correction: the anchor is an image, whereas a WAM accepts only
an observation and an instruction. Moreover, it leans on visual context and largely ignores the
instruction: with the observation fixed, the original and the empty instruction yield nearly identical
attention maps (Figure 1). The anchor must therefore be conveyed through a corrective instruction,
and the influence of that instruction must be strengthened to elicit the intended correction.
For challenge (1), we introduce the Anchor Verifier, which compares each observation with its an-
chor together with the executed actions and tracks the mismatch along the chunk. It thus responds
to deviations that break the task rather than to any visual difference.
For challenge (2), we introduce Anchor-Guided Recovery to return the scene to a state the policy
knows how to continue from. It hands the alarmed anchor, the current observation, and the task to a
vision-language model, which issues one short instruction naming the condition to restore rather than
the pixels to reproduce. The frozen WAM then executes it under strengthened instruction guidance
to reach the intended future before deviation.
We evaluate FAVOR on LIBERO [38] and LIBERO-Plus [16]. FAVOR raises the task success of
the base WAM from 97.85% to 98.10% on LIBERO and from 72.60% to 72.98% on LIBERO-Plus.
The Anchor Verifier separates failed from successful episodes with an AUROC of 0.992, above
distance-based and learned detectors [17, 72].
In summary, our main contributions are as follows:
• A future-anchored view of WAM execution: the future predicted before acting serves not
only as a reference for verifying execution but also as the target that recovery should restore.
• The Anchor Verifier, which grounds execution verification in the WAM’s own predicted
future and executed actions, identifying when the scene no longer supports continuation.
• The Anchor-Guided Recovery, which translates the alarmed anchor into a corrective in-
struction and strengthens instruction guidance, enabling a WAM to use its own predicted
future as a recovery goal and return to a state from which the original task can continue.

RELATED WORK
2.1
WORLD ACTION MODELS AND FUTURE PREDICTION
WAMs integrate future prediction into robot control [56]. Joint video–action models couple antici-
pated visual changes with robot actions in a single generative process [5, 27, 32, 35, 57, 74, 75, 86,
87], whereas cascaded interfaces pass visual future states as explicit conditions to inverse dynamics
models (IDMs) [3, 12, 23, 28, 36, 62, 69]. The role of prediction depends on the inference inter-
face: Fast-WAM separates the benefit of video co-training from that of explicit future generation at
test time [78]. Related efforts use video models as simulators or reward sources [15, 20, 73] and
diagnose whether generated futures follow the commanded actions [6, 33, 42]. We focus on WAMs
whose predicted future states condition action generation and thus accompany the resulting actions.
In these interfaces, and in work on aligning visual features and instructions with action decoding [21,
44, 53], the predicted future is an input to action generation and plays no further role once the actions
are produced. FFDC is the exception: it retains the prediction and compares it with incoming
observations to judge whether the remaining actions can still be trusted, shortening execution or
replanning when they cannot [64]. Dense correspondence from long-range flow or all-pairs point
tracking [65, 68] could also relate the two frames, but it measures motion rather than task relevance.
HarnessWAM also intervenes during WAM execution, but monitors subgoal completion rather than
the prediction [18]. Here, as in adaptive-horizon methods [48, 63, 64], the signal only decides
whether to continue, and replanning starts from the deviated state.
2.2
EXECUTION VERIFICATION AND RECOVERY
Execution monitoring for learned policies draws on internal features, action consistency, and se-
mantic progress [1, 13, 14, 17, 34, 43, 47, 72, 79, 84]. Diagnostics for world models further expose
inconsistency between imagination and execution, from rollouts that violate physical and causal
consistency [7] to actions that diverge from a correctly imagined future [33]. A detector, however,
says only that something went wrong, not what state to return to.
Existing recovery methods obtain their target from outside the acting policy. Planner-level loops
revise behavior from environmental feedback and constraint violations [19, 24, 85], and test-time
language correction takes the instruction from a human [39, 40, 58]. Policy-level recovery for VLAs
is learned from failure-and-recovery data [11, 31, 45, 46] or inferred by an auxiliary predictive
component, either a dynamics model [50] or imagined states toward which recovery is directed [59,
83]. Each obtains its objective at the cost of training or a second model, and none derives it from
the plan the policy was executing. FAVOR takes the objective from that plan: the future the WAM
predicted before acting is what recovery restores. Since a WAM accepts only an observation and an
instruction, the VLM only translates this future into an instruction rather than estimating a target of
its own. This translation step draws on VLM work in task-directed visual perception [76] and spatial
reasoning [71, 80]. However, formulating an appropriate corrective instruction does not ensure that
the WAM will act on it.
In the WAM considered here, an IDM decodes actions from the predicted future, so the influence
of a corrective instruction on execution depends largely on how it shapes future generation. Yet
the WAM used here exhibits limited sensitivity to instruction changes, producing largely unchanged
outputs across different instructions (Figure 1). On the policy side, prior work makes corrections
effective by training on them [39, 40, 58] or by aligning visual features and instructions with action
decoding [21, 44, 53]. On the generation side, classifier-free guidance strengthens a condition at
inference time [22], and controllable generation and editing steer diffusion models with visual con-
ditions [10, 30, 52, 60, 66, 67, 70, 81] or instructions [77]. FAVOR strengthens the instruction on
the generation side at inference time: guidance makes it shape the generated future, and the IDM
turns that future into recovery actions, without retraining.

METHOD
Overview.
As observed in Section 1, the future predicted before acting depicts the states the chunk
was meant to reach, and these states lie where the policy is reliable. FAVOR therefore keeps the
predicted frames as anchors and uses them twice (Figure 2). An Anchor Verifier checks whether
the current observation has deviated too far from its anchor, and Anchor-Guided Recovery uses that
anchor to guide the robot back on course.

Training data.
To train the Anchor Verifier, we construct two datasets with different levels of
supervision. The first is a locally labeled set with a deviation label at every anchor. From the
same simulator state, we execute a clean and a perturbed version of the planned actions and label
each anchor by whether the perturbed execution has departed from the clean one. We use two
perturbations, late Gaussian noise added to the arm commands in the latter half of the chunk and tail
scaling that multiplies these commands by a fixed factor. The second is an episode-labeled set that
carries only a terminal success or failure label per episode; it contains natural rollouts of the policy
together with the demonstrations from its training set, which all count as successes.

ANCHOR-GUIDED RECOVERY
At the alarm, the controller discards the unexecuted actions of the chunk and keeps the alarmed an-
chor Ianc. The WAM is conditioned on a current observation and an instruction, so the anchor needs
to reach it through language. Specifically, we use a VLM M to generate a corrective instruction for
transitioning from the current observation It⋆to the anchored state Ianc.
ℓcorr = M(Ianc, It⋆, ℓ).

The anchor specifies the nominal state that execution was expected to reach. Expressing this target

generates futures and actions from the current observation and ℓcorr, exactly as in normal execution.

EXPERIMENTS
5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Benchmarks and evaluation protocols.
We evaluate FAVOR on LIBERO [38] and LIBERO-
Plus [16].
LIBERO comprises four suites of ten tasks each: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long
(LIBERO-10). LIBERO-Plus uses the LIBERO tasks while varying the environment, task con-
figuration, and observation distribution beyond the standard setting. We run 100 rollouts per task on
LIBERO and a total of 1698 rollouts on LIBERO-Plus.
Implementation details.
We instantiate FAVOR on the IDM variant of Fast-WAM [78]. The
Anchor Verifier is trained on one NVIDIA A5000 GPU (24 GB). We use Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct [2]
as the VLM in Anchor-Guided Recovery. All evaluations run on four A5000 GPUs.
Evaluation metrics.
The primary metric is native task success, determined by the environment’s
own success condition and computed over the complete evaluation cohort, including episodes in
which no intervention occurs. It thus credits successful recoveries and penalizes interventions that
disrupt otherwise successful execution. The Anchor Verifier and Anchor-Guided Recovery are ad-
ditionally evaluated in isolation in Section 5.3.
5.2
MAIN RESULTS
Results on LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus.
Table 1 reports native task success. FAVOR raises the
average success of the base WAM from 97.85% to 98.10% on LIBERO, cutting the failure rate by
11.6% relative, and from 72.60% to 72.98% on LIBERO-Plus. Both components are needed. From
the same alarms, direct replanning under the original instruction removes only 3.3% of the base
failures on LIBERO and none on LIBERO-Plus. Language correction without the anchor removes
7.9% on LIBERO but slightly hurts on LIBERO-Plus (72.53%); supplying the anchor accounts for
the rest. The gain is largest on Object and Long. Object tasks share one scene and differ only in the
target object, so the policy relies more on the instruction and a corrective instruction moves it more;
on Long, an early deviation has the most time to compound.
Qualitative results.
Figure 4 shows two cases that fail under native continuation and are rescued
by FAVOR. In both, the Anchor Verifier detects the deviated state as soon as the observation stops
matching its anchor: a bottle that the plan expects in the gripper but is not, and a mug that has left
its intended path. Anchor-Guided Recovery reads the anchor to identify what was lost and issues a
short instruction that restores it, “pick up the orange juice bottle” and “take the white mug”. The
scene returns to the anchored state, the original plan applies again, and both tasks complete. Native
continuation, by contrast, keeps executing the remaining actions as if nothing had happened, and
both tasks fail once the budget runs out.

5.3
ABLATION STUDIES
Anchor Verifier.
We compare the Anchor Verifier with DINO adaptations of SAFE-MLP and
SAFE-LSTM [17], and FAIL-Detect logpZO [72], as well as DINO feature distance based on DI-
NOv2 [49] and PSNR [25]. All six methods score the same saved base-WAM test episodes, whose
initial states are disjoint from those used to train, validate, and calibrate the learned detectors.
Each episode is scored by its maximum anomaly score over the anchors of complete chunks. The
baselines split into learned detectors that do not see the anchor and fixed comparisons with it. SAFE-
MLP and SAFE-LSTM [17] learn a failure classifier from terminal labels on the same DINO features
of the observation, per anchor and over the anchor sequence respectively. FAIL-Detect logpZO [72]
fits a flow model to the observation features of successful episodes and scores an episode by how
unlikely its features are under that model. DINO distance averages Euclidean differences between
corresponding observed and anchor tokens, and PSNR uses the mean squared RGB error between
the observation and the anchor across both cameras, negated so that higher scores indicate greater
discrepancy; neither is trained. Table 2 reports episode-level AUROC and AUPRC with task failure
as the positive class, and failure coverage at each detector’s own threshold, calibrated on successful
episodes at a 5% alarm rate and fixed before testing.
The Anchor Verifier leads on all three metrics. The fixed comparisons rank episodes well (AUROC
0.94–0.96) but have low AUPRC and coverage, so a raw distance to the anchor cannot tell a lost
grasp from a harmless mismatch. The learned detectors without the anchor do no better, and SAFE-
LSTM collapses. Learning the comparison against the anchor combines the two and raises AUPRC
by 0.12 and coverage by 24 points. Figure 5 shows why. Under every baseline, the scores of
successful episodes spread over the same range as those of failures. Under the Anchor Verifier, the

Anchor-Guided Recovery.
We ablate the guidance scale of Anchor-Guided Recovery: Figure 3
fixes the state and the corrective instruction “pick up the cup” and varies only γ in Eq. (13). At
γ = 1, which reduces to plain conditional sampling, the predicted future is nearly identical to the
continuation under the original task: the correction is ignored. At γ = 3 the prediction begins to
depart from that continuation, but it does not yet realize the instruction, as the arm leaves the original
path without reaching a grasp; the correction is registered but not followed. At γ = 5 the predicted
future shows the arm grasping the cup, and the IDM turns this future into the corresponding recovery
actions. The guidance scale thus sets how far the corrective instruction overrides the policy’s habitual
continuation, and it must be large enough for the instruction to prevail.
6
CONCLUSION
World action models plan by predicting the future, yet once execution drifts from that plan they have
no way to notice or to recover. FAVOR adds this capability to a frozen WAM by reusing its own
prediction. The Anchor Verifier compares observations with their anchors to detect deviations that
break the task, and Anchor-Guided Recovery turns the alarmed anchor into a corrective instruction
that steers the policy back to a state it can continue from. On LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus it reduces
failures and improves end-to-end success without modifying the policy.

Training data.
We construct paired execution snippets and complete policy rollouts. For each
paired snippet, we save a simulator state and use the frozen policy to generate a 16-step action
sequence and its imagined visual trajectory. We restore the same state for clean and perturbed
executions, sharing the original imagined reference. Perturbations either add Gaussian noise to the
arm commands or scale their magnitudes over the last eight steps, while leaving gripper commands
unchanged. We record both branches’ observations and physical states to derive local deviation
labels from their object and end-effector displacements.

For complete rollouts, we execute the frozen policy until success or the action budget is exhausted.
We record the task instruction, initial-state identifier and random seed, imagined frames and planned
actions at each replanning point, executed actions, synchronized external and wrist-camera observa-
tions, robot and object states, and the terminal task outcome. Observations are aligned with imagined
frames after actions 4, 8, 12, and 16 of each chunk; incomplete trailing chunks are excluded. Paired
snippets provide local deviation labels, whereas complete rollouts provide terminal outcomes.
Episode-level training.
The episode-level stage warm-starts from a checkpoint previously trained
with paired data and localized labels and optimizes Lep in Eq. (11); the episode maximum spans
the valid anchors of all complete chunks, and padding contributes to no loss term. Each epoch visits
every training success once, batching up to 7 successes with one cyclically sampled failure, so that
all training failures are covered. We use AdamW with a learning rate of 10−5, weight decay of 10−4,
and gradient norm clipping at 1.0 for 5 epochs.
Locally supervised refinement.
The episode maximum may become high only after an error has
become irreversible. To study temporal specificity, a training variant supplements terminal-outcome
supervision with reviewed anchor labels:
Llocal = λLF + LS
λ + 1
+ ηLann.
(14)
Here λ weights failed episodes and η weights the auxiliary local-label loss. This refinement changes
training but not the inference architecture.
Specifically, Lann = 0.5L+ + 0.5Levent + L−+ 0.2Lrank + 0.1Lhard. The per-anchor losses L+
and L−are binary cross-entropy on reviewed positive and negative anchors, averaged within each
annotated episode and then across episodes. For the event term Levent, events are contiguous runs
of positive anchors within a chunk, pooled from logits u by pool(u) = 0.5 log mean exp(2u). The
ranking term averages sp(0.5 −pool(u+) + pool(u−)) against reviewed-negative bags from the
same task, including the same episode. The hard-negative term averages the negative-target binary
cross-entropy over the four highest reviewed-negative logits of each episode, or over all of them if
fewer exist. Successful episodes are excluded from this term, and empty terms contribute zero.

Empty-instruction control.
For the language analysis in Section 4.2, the literal empty string by-
passes the usual instruction template. Its cached text encoding retains one valid special token, and
