## Abstract 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models have shown strong potential as generalist robot policies, but adapting them to unseen tasks often requires costly parameter updates. Recent work such as RICL introduces in-context adaptability by retrieving expert demonstrations based on the current VLA observation and providing them as additional context at test time. The effectiveness of this adaptation therefore depends critically on the retrieval mechanism. In this work, we systematically study how different retrieval methods affect both retrieval quality and task performance within the RICL framework. Specifically, we compare four different methods: image-based retrieval, retrieval augmented with VLA’s state, retrieval using fea-tures from the VLA backbone, and random retrieval. Our experiments yield three main findings. First, no retrieval method consistently dominates the others in task success, while surprisingly, random retrieval achieves a non-trivial success rate. Second, standard retrieval-quality diagnostics do not reliably reflect downstream VLA performance. Third, demonstrations from different but related tasks can provide useful transferable information. Together, these results provide an initial step toward understanding how retrieval mechanisms shape the in-context learning capability of VLA models and their downstream task performance, while highlight-ing the need for more careful design and evaluation of retrieval mechanisms for reliable test-time adaptation. 

## 1 Introduction 

Recent advances in vision-language-action (VLA) models [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8] have enabled a single policy to perform a broad range of robotic manipulation tasks, demonstrating their potential as general-purpose robotic control policies. However, even strong pre-trained VLAs often require substantial fine-tuning or retraining to perform reliably under distribution shifts, such as new environments, unseen objects, or novel task instructions [9], which limits rapid adaptation at deployment. In contrast, large language models (LLMs) [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18] can often adapt to new tasks at inference time through in-context learning (ICL): given only a few input-output examples as context, an LLM can adapt to a new task without updating its parameters [19, 20, 21]. Inspired by this paradigm, RICL [22] equips a pre-trained VLA with in-context adaptability by conditioning action prediction on previously collected expert demonstrations, enabling adaptation to unseen tasks without test-time parameter updates. Specifically, RICL maintains a small retrieval buffer of expert demonstrations and, given the current observation, retrieves relevant demonstrations as additional context for the VLA policy. The effectiveness of such retrieval-based in-context adaptation therefore

depends critically on which demonstrations are selected from the retrieval buffer, since the retrieved examples determine what task-specific information is provided to the VLA at test time. In this work, we study how the current observation should be represented for retrieval so that the returned demonstrations are most useful for adapting to an unseen task. The original RICL framework performs retrieval using only the embedding of the current image. We investigate whether incorporat-ing additional information, such as the robot’s proprioceptive state or features extracted directly from the VLA backbone, can lead to more effective retrieval and improved task performance. Our main findings are: 1. No retrieval representation clearly dominates. Surprisingly, even random retrieval achieves a non-trivial success rate, suggesting that the VLA can still benefit from demonstrations even when they are not selected based on similarity to the current observation. 2. Better retrieval quality does not necessarily translate into better downstream control performance. 3. Retrieval from different but related tasks can provide useful transferable information. Together, these results suggest that improving retrieval for VLA adaptation requires going beyond simple similarity matching between the current observation and stored demonstrations, as well as straightforward extensions such as directly incorporating additional information. More importantly, retrieval-quality metrics should be designed to better reflect their effectiveness during actual VLA execution.

## 3 Empirical Study of Retrieval for VLA Adaptation 

In this section, we conduct an empirical study to answer the following research questions: (1) Which retrieval representation works best? (2) Do standard retrieval-quality diagnostics reliably predict downstream control performance? (3) Is retrieval from a different task necessarily harmful?

Evaluation Benchmark. We evaluate all methods on the LIBERO benchmark [24], which was not considered in the original RICL study. LIBERO is a simulated robotic manipulation benchmark built around the Franka Panda arm. For retrieval, we construct a shared demonstration buffer containing 20 expert demonstrations per task, and all retrieval methods operate on this same buffer. For evaluation, we select 25 evaluation tasks, and each task is tested on 10 fixed initial scenes that are disjoint from the demonstration scenes used to construct the retrieval buffer. 

Model Architecture. We evaluate all retrieval methods using a fixed π0-FAST VLA model [3], following the RICL setting. Since the original RICL model is trained for a different benchmark, we further train the model on LIBERO using a comparable RICL post-training procedure. 

Retrieval Methods. We compare four retrieval methods. RICL-Image follows the original RICL setting and retrieves demonstrations using only the DINOv2 embedding of the top-camera image. 

RICL-ImageState augments image-based retrieval with the proprioceptive state st, allowing the retrieval score to account for both visual and robot-state similarity. We include this method to evaluate whether incorporating state information improves retrieval. RICL-VLAFeat performs retrieval using features extracted directly from the π0-FAST model, taking the top-camera image and proprioceptive state as inputs. This method tests whether representations learned by the VLA itself provide a more effective retrieval than generic visual features. RICL-Random samples entries uniformly from the demonstration buffer and serves as a baseline for comparison. All methods retrieve M = 4 entries from the same demonstration buffer. 

3.2 Results No retrieval representation clearly dominates. Table 1 reports the main results for the four retrieval methods. In addition to the overall success rate, we report a task-hierarchical bootstrap confidence interval, denoted as task-hier. CI . This interval captures task-level uncertainty. Since a method may achieve a high average success rate by performing well on some tasks while failing on others, task-level bootstrapping therefore provides a more conservative estimate of how reliably a retrieval method performs across different tasks. Details of the bootstrap procedure are provided in Appendix D. We also conduct pairwise comparisons between the retrieval methods. For each comparison, we use an exact paired McNemar test [25, 26] detailed in Appendix D and report the resulting p-value. A smaller 

p-value provides stronger evidence that the two methods have different success rates. Table 1 shows 3that, considering success rate alone, RICL-VLAFeat achieves the highest performance at 40 .4% ,slightly outperforming RICL-Image at 40 .0% and RICL-ImageState at 36 .8% . This suggests that features extracted from the VLA backbone can provide an effective retrieval representation without relying on an additional DINOv2 encoder. Interestingly, augmenting image-based retrieval with proprioceptive state does not improve performance and instead leads to a lower numerical success rate. However, these differences are small: the pairwise comparisons among the three retrieval methods yield large McNemar-p values. Thus, none of the retrieval methods can be shown to statistically outperform the others. Another notable observation is the relatively strong performance of RICL-Random , which achieves a 26 .0% success rate despite retrieving demonstrations uniformly at random. This suggests that VLA can still benefit from retrieved demonstrations even when they are not selected based on similarity to the current query, or that its behavior remains partially robust to irrelevant retrieved context. Retrieval quality does not reliably predict control performance. To understand how re-trieval quality relates to the actual behavior of the VLA, we consider two complementary mea-surements, both based on the assumption that demonstrations from the same task are more likely to provide directly relevant information. We first measure the exact-task retrieval rate, de-fined as the fraction of retrieved entries whose task identity matches that of the query. We eval-uate this metric in two settings: an offline setting, where queries are expert states from the same evaluation scenes, and an on-policy setting, where queries are the states actually visited by the VLA during execution. This comparison reveals how retrieval behavior changes when moving from success-ful expert trajectories to the states encountered by the policy itself. We additionally use Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG) [27] to evaluate retrieval relevance in the offline setting, where a retrieved frame is considered more relevant if it comes from the same task and its subsequent action chunk is more similar to that of the query state. Additional implementation details are provided in Appendix D. Table 2 shows a substantial gap between offline and on-policy exact-task retrieval. When queried offline using expert frames, all three retrieval methods return same-task demonstrations in more than 80% of the retrieved entries. However, this rate drops markedly when the query comes from states actually visited by the VLA during execution. This suggests that retrieval behavior measured offline does not directly transfer to the states encountered by the policy during execution. A similar mismatch appears when comparing offline NDCG with task success. RICL-ImageState achieves the highest offline NDCG of 0.7897 , followed by RICL-Image at 0.7461 and RICL-VLAFeat at 

0.7280 . However, as shown in Table 1, RICL-ImageState has the lowest success rate among the three retrieval methods, while RICL-VLAFeat achieves the highest numerical success rate. These results indicate that better retrieval quality does not necessarily translate into better downstream control performance. 

Retrieval from different tasks can provide useful information. We further examine representative retrieval traces to understand whether retrieving demonstrations from a different task necessarily indi-cates retrieval failure. As shown in Figure 1, in one successful example on Task 52 for RICL-Image ,the dominant retrieved source shifts across Task 50, Task 49, Task 46, and eventually the same Task 52 during task execution. This example shows that the VLA can exploit demonstrations from multiple related tasks that share transferable manipulation structure, rather than relying exclusively on exact-task retrieval. Additional examples can be found in Appendix E. In contrast, we also observe systematic retrieval between tasks with similar scene structure but different goals, such as Task 24 (put the black bowl in the bottom drawer of the cabinet) and Task 25 (put the black bowl on top of the cabinet), as shown in Appendix E, Figure 3. Although these two tasks involve the same object and environment, they require different target locations. This suggests that retrieval from a different task 4should not be automatically regarded as incorrect. Its usefulness depends on whether the retrieved demonstration provides action-relevant information consistent with the current task goal.

6A Conclusion 

In this work, we study how different retrieval mechanisms affect in-context adaptation for vision-language-action models within the RICL framework. Our results show that no retrieval representation consistently dominates the others in downstream task success, while random retrieval still achieves a non-trivial success rate. We further find that standard retrieval-quality diagnostics do not reliably predict downstream control performance, as retrieval behavior changes substantially when moving from expert states to the states actually encountered by the VLA. Finally, our qualitative analysis shows that demonstrations from different but related tasks can still provide useful transferable information. Together, these findings suggest that effective retrieval for VLA adaptation requires more than simple similarity matching or straightforwardly adding additional observation information. Future retrieval mechanisms should instead better account for whether a retrieved demonstration is actually useful for the current control decision, and retrieval-quality metrics should more faithfully reflect the behavior of the VLA during actual execution.

## C Related Work 

While recent vision-language-action (VLA) models, such as OpenVLA [2] and π0 [1], have demon-strated strong generalization through pretraining on large and diverse robot datasets, adapting them to new tasks often still requires task-specific fine-tuning or retraining, limiting their ability to behave as truly generalist agents. In contrast, humans can rapidly adapt to novel tasks by drawing on prior experience [28, 29, 30]. Motivated by this capability, recent work has explored in-context imitation learning , in which demonstrations of a new task are provided directly to the VLA at inference time, enabling adaptation without updating the model parameters. ICRT [31] is an early representative approach, formulating robot control as next-token prediction over sequences of visual observations, proprioceptive states, and actions. By placing one or more human teleoperation trajectories in the transformer’s context, ICRT can infer and execute previously unseen tasks without test-time parameter updates, demonstrating that the in-context learning behavior of autoregressive models can extend from language to continuous robotic control. More recently, RICL [22] showed that such capability does not naturally emerge in pretrained VLAs and proposed explicitly post-training a pretrained 

π0-FAST policy to exploit retrieved demonstrations. At deployment, RICL retrieves relevant states and actions from a small demonstration buffer and incorporates them into the model context, enabling adaptation to new manipulation tasks using roughly 10–20 demonstrations without updating model parameters. A parallel direction investigates whether existing frozen VLAs can benefit from demonstrations without additional in-context learning (ICL) training. Retrieval-VLA [9] enables training-free adaptation by identifying relevant past experience and directly incorporates the associated information into the prediction of a frozen VLA. Since the retrieved demonstrations are not provided as context that the VLA itself must learn to interpret, this approach avoids ICL-specific post-training. Later work further enriches the information provided by demonstrations. For example, StellaVLA [32] converts retrieved trajectories into structured task plans, subgoals, and motion descriptions before conditioning the VLA on them, rather than relying only on raw trajectory similarity. Collectively, these studies establish demonstrations and retrieval as a promising interface for rapidly adapting VLAs without task-specific parameter updates.

Task-hierarchical bootstrap confidence interval. To quantify uncertainty in the overall task success rate, we use a task-hierarchical bootstrap procedure that treats tasks, rather than individual evaluation trials, as the primary sampling units. Each of the 25 evaluation tasks is tested on 10 fixed initial scenes. For a given retrieval method, we first compute the success rate separately for each task by averaging the binary success outcomes over its 10 evaluation scenes. This produces 25 task-level success rates. We then generate one bootstrap sample by sampling 25 tasks with replacement from these 25 task-level results. For the sampled tasks, we compute the mean of their task-level success rates to obtain one bootstrapped estimate of the overall success rate. We repeat this procedure for 10,000 bootstrap samples, resulting in an empirical distribution of the overall task success rate. The 

task-hier. CI is defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of this bootstrap distribution, corresponding to a 95% confidence interval. 

McNemar test. To compare two retrieval methods under the same evaluation conditions, we use the exact McNemar test [25, 26]. Since all methods are evaluated on the same tasks and the same fixed initial scenes, their success and failure outcomes can be compared pairwise. For two methods 

A and B, let n10 denote the number of evaluations for which A succeeds while B fails, and let n01 

denote the number for which A fails while B succeeds. Evaluations on which both methods succeed or both fail do not provide evidence about their relative performance and are therefore not used by the McNemar test. Under the null hypothesis that the two methods have the same success rate, the two disagreement cases should be equally likely: A succeeds while B fails, or B succeeds while A fails. Let nd = n10 + n01 be the total number of disagreements. Conditioning on these nd disagreement cases, the number of cases favoring method A follows a binomial distribution, 

N10 ∼ Binomial( nd, 1/2) ,

under the null hypothesis. The two-sided exact McNemar p-value is therefore 

p = 2 min {Pr( N10 ≤ n10 ), Pr( N10 ≥ n10 )} ,

clipped at 1. A small p-value indicates that the difference between n10 and n01 is statistically significant, providing evidence that methods A and B have different success rates. 

Retrieval-quality analysis. To understand how retrieval quality affects the actual behavior of the VLA, we first measure the exact-task retrieval rate under two different query settings. Specifically, for a given query, the exact-task retrieval rate is defined as the fraction of the M retrieved entries whose task identity matches that of the query task. This metric reflects how often the retriever returns demonstrations from the same task, under the assumption that same-task demonstrations are more likely to provide directly relevant information for executing the target task. We first consider an 

offline setting. For each of the 10 fixed evaluation scenes per task, we use states sampled from the corresponding successful expert demonstration as retrieval queries and compute the exact-task retrieval rate. We then consider a matched on-policy setting using the same initial scenes, but query the retrieval buffer with the states actually visited by the VLA during execution. Since the expert and on-policy queries originate from the same initial scenes, this comparison isolates how retrieval behavior changes when moving from successful expert trajectories to the states encountered by the policy itself.
