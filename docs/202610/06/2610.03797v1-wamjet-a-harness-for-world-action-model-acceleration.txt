# WAMJET: A Harness for World Action Model Acceleration 

Le Chen 1, Lixin Liu 2,†, Jan Schneider 1, Zeju Qiu 1, Simon Guist 1, Bernhard Sch¨ olkopf 1, Dieter B¨ uchler 1,3

Abstract — World Action Models (WAMs) leverage pretrained video foundation models for robot manipulation, but their large backbones and video-action co-prediction are expensive. Although existing acceleration techniques offer many ways to reduce this cost, selecting and composing them requires substantial engineering for each model and hardware platform. To tackle this bottleneck, we present WAMJET, an agentic harness that accelerates WAM inference by equipping coding agents with reusable optimization guidance and measurement and validation tools. WAMJET follows a bottleneck-driven workflow where the agent profiles inference, modifies targeted code, validates effects, and iteratively refines the acceleration stack as bottlenecks shift, while preserving action quality. Experiments span six WAMs, three coding agents, and two GPU architectures. WAMJET achieves up to 9.95× lossless speedup over upstream implementations. Approximation and hardware-aware optimization yield additional latency reductions, with comparable success rates. The results show that WAMJET can produce effective acceleration stacks for WAM deployment. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Building generalist policies requires not only mapping visual observations to actions but also understanding and predicting the consequences of actions in diverse environ-ments. Video generation models learn representations of such dynamics from large-scale video datasets. Many of these videos contain intentional behavior like object manipulation and scene changes because someone or something acted. That makes video models attractive backbones for generalist manip-ulation. Before seeing any robot actions, the backbone already encodes useful links between language, visual change, and plausible object interactions. World Action Models (WAMs) build on these models and extend them with action-generation capabilities. They inherit priors from video pretraining and couple action generation with video-based world modeling for more robust robot manipulation across diverse environments. Compared with Vision-Language-Action Models (VLAs), WAMs are appealing because predicting the future states of the environment may help capture better physical dynamics and temporal information. Modern video generation models are often billion-scale parameter models and continue to scale toward higher generation quality and higher fidelity. This scaling provides stronger priors about how visual scenes evolve but also increases inference cost. The same backbones that make

WAMs attractive also make them expensive to run. Inference may involve sampling a large video model, several denoising steps, and repeated vision-language processing. Beyond those computations, data movement, synchronization, and cache updates can also add substantial latency. This computational burden poses a significant challenge for real-time control in robotics, where timely decision-making is crucial. Many techniques can accelerate inference, including compi-lation, kernel fusion, caching, and quantization. The difficulty is deciding which techniques to apply and how to combine them. A model dominated by repeated conditioning needs different optimizations from one governed by attention or memory transfers. Hardware also affects which kernels or data types are efficient. Moreover, optimizations can interact, which means removing one bottleneck can expose another, and a change that initially gives little benefit may become useful later. Developing an effective acceleration strategy, therefore, requires repeated profiling, implementation, and measurement for each deployment. Preserving the policy’s behavior adds another challenge. Reusing a prompt embedding while the instruction is unchanged can avoid redundant computation. Reusing visual features after the observation changes, how-ever, approximates the policy’s input. Quantization can introduce further error, whose effect on manipulation may be difficult to predict from tensor differences alone. An optimization process must account for both inference speed and the effect of its changes on robot actions. Accelerating WAM inference is not a one-time optimization but requires a recipe that composes techniques for a specific model, hardware stack, and task. The best recipe for a certain WAM may not directly transfer to other models. As illustrated in Fig. 1, hand-crafting an acceleration strategy that works well on a specific model and hardware setup requires substantial engineering effort and may not generalize to other scenarios. LLM coding agents offer a way to automate this process, but reliable optimization over many iterations requires clear constraints, appropriate tools, and feedback on both latency and correctness. Harness engineering provides this structure through agent-readable workflows, measurement tools, and explicit validation criteria [1]. This shifts human effort toward designing reusable guidance, while the agent conducts experiments, assesses their outcomes, and uses the evidence to guide subsequent optimizations. In this work, we present WAMJET, an agentic harness for accelerating WAM inference. WAMJET equips coding agents with optimization guidance and tools for profiling, latency measurement, numerical comparison, and paired task analysis. A. Foundation Models for Robot Manipulation 

Foundation models support robot manipulation through both direct action prediction and higher-level decision mak-ing. RT-2 transfers vision-language pretraining into control by representing robot actions as tokens, while OpenVLA provides an open-source vision-language-action (VLA) model trained on diverse robot demonstrations [2], [3]. π0 combines a pretrained vision-language backbone with flow-matching action generation, and Octo learns a generalist diffusion policy from heterogeneous robot data [4], [5]. Other approaches leverage language models: SayCan selects skills based on learned affordances [6], while Code as Policies generates executable programs for robot control [7]. A related line connects policy learning with future visual prediction. UniPi formulates planning as text-conditioned video generation and extracts executable actions from the resulting visual plans [8]. Unified models couple video and action learning: UVA learns a shared latent representation with decoupled diffusion heads, while UWM integrates video and action diffusion in a single transformer with independently controlled diffusion timesteps [9], [10]. DreamZero builds on a pretrained video diffusion backbone to jointly predict future observations and robot actions [11]. WAMJET accelerates the inference code of a trained policy, with numerical and task evaluations used to assess the effect of each optimization. 

B. Accelerating Video Generation and World Action Models 

Diffusion acceleration operates at several levels of the inference computation. DPM-Solver reduces the number of model evaluations through higher-order numerical integration, whereas progressive distillation trains a student to reproduce a longer sampling process with fewer steps [12], [13]. Training-free caching methods instead exploit redundancy along the denoising trajectory: TeaCache uses timestep-conditioned input changes to guide reuse, and TaylorSeer forecasts intermediate features rather than simply reusing them [14], [15]. These approximations differ from implementations such as FlashAttention, which accelerates exact dense attention through IO-aware tiling and reduced memory traffic [19]. WAM-specific works explore both architectural efficiency and deployment-time optimization. DreamZero combines caching, quantization, compilation, kernel optimization, and asynchronous execution in a model-specific control stack [11]. WAMJET builds on these techniques, selecting and combining them according to the bottlenecks of each model and hardware platform. 

C. Agentic Workflows and Harness Engineering 

Agentic systems make iterative tool use and external feedback central to problem-solving. ReAct introduced the rea-soning–action loop [23], SWE-agent built an agent–computer interface for solving software engineering tasks [24], and agent harnesses go further by designing the whole runtime system around the agent [25]. In performance engineering, CUDA-LLM and CudaForge use executable correctness checks and hardware-level performance measurements to iteratively improve GPU kernels [26], [27]. Robotics harnesses apply these ideas to different stages of the robot-learning lifecycle. HARBOR automates robot RL training by coordinating specialized agents from environment setup to reward tuning and to policy training [28]. Nau-tilus standardizes policy-benchmark-robot integration through typed contracts and validated workflows [29]. Show-Harness instead operates within the embodied control loop: it exposes compact semantic actions to a VLM and deterministically maps them into robot motion, with execution feedback from observations [30]. WAMJET instead applies agentic optimization to the inference code of a trained WAM. The most related framework is the Sol Video Inference Engine [31], which optimizes video generation by assigning caching, sparse attention, token pruning, quantization, and kernel fusion to parallel agents, then integrating results using human quality feedback. WAMJET addresses the additional requirements of action generation: optimizations must preserve the policy’s recurrent state, respond correctly to changing observations, and be evaluated for their effect on manipulation. Our workflow combines bottleneck analysis with numerical and stateful checks and uses paired task evaluation to assess approximations. III. P RELIMINARIES 

A visuomotor policy maps current observations plus a goal or instruction to robot actions. A world model predicts future visual or latent states from the current state plus an action or goal abstraction. A WAM sits at the overlap: it leverages a pretrained video generation world model backbone as a prior and predicts both future states and robot actions. We consider robot manipulation from visual observations and language instructions. At time step t, let xt = ( ot, q t, l )

denote the visual observation, proprioceptive state, and language instruction. A standard policy models the conditional distribution over an action chunk at = ( at, . . . , a t+H−1) of horizon H given xt:

at ∼ pθ (at | xt) (1) where θ denotes the policy parameters. WAMs learn to predict robot actions alongside future states. Let st = ( st+1 , . . . , s t+T ) denote future video frames or their latent representations over a horizon T . WAMs jointly generate both future states and actions: 

(st, at) ∼ pθ (st, at | xt). (2) The joint distribution can be factorized as 

pθ (st, at | xt) = pθ (st | xt) · pθ (at | xt, st) (3) where future states are first imagined by the video backbone, and actions are then inferred conditioned on the imagined future, so that the action distribution effectively marginalizes over imagined futures rather than mapping the current context to actions directly. In practice, the inference procedure depends on the architecture; this factorization is instantiated either by jointly denoising future frames and actions within a shared model [11], or by first generating future video and then feeding it to a separate inverse dynamics module [32], [20]. The expensive stages, therefore, differ across WAMs, even when they share the same video model backbone. The dominant cost may lie in visual encoding, repeated denois-ing, action generation, or the transfers and synchronization between these stages. It depends on the architecture and deployment platform, so a technique that helps one WAM might not help another. IV. WAMJET 

A. Overview 

WAMJET is a harness that guides a coding agent in accelerating a trained WAM policy. Its optimization skill describes how to establish a baseline, identify bottlenecks, test changes, and validate results. Python tools support this workflow through inference timing, trace analysis, numerical comparisons, and paired task analysis. Table I summarizes these components by functional role. The user specifies the policy code and checkpoint, inference configuration, quality requirements, and search budget. Within these constraints, the agent searches for a faster implementation, recording each experiment and its accept/revert decision. It returns the selected implementation with the measurements and validation evidence needed to reproduce and assess the result. As demonstrated in Fig. 2, WAMJET guides the agent through an iterative bottleneck-driven optimization work-flow. The agent first establishes a runnable baseline and reduces startup costs to allow more experiments within the budget. It then profiles inference to identify bottlenecks, explores corresponding lossless acceleration techniques, and benchmarks each candidate under comparable conditions. Experiment records guide subsequent trials, while reusable findings are retained in external memory. When approximation is permitted, the agent also explores approximate techniques, accepting candidates only after the required checks establish that they satisfy the user’s quality requirements. After each change, the agent reassesses the remaining bottlenecks and continues searching until it meets the user requirements or reaches the budget limit. B. Agent-Native Acceleration Workflow 

Establish a baseline. The agent uses environment preflight checks to establish a runnable inference call on the target GPU and resolves any compatibility issues. The same fixes are applied to both the baseline and subsequent candidates. It then saves the original outputs and measures latency over repeated calls after warmup, keeping the checkpoint, inputs, random seeds, and inference settings fixed. Repeated baseline outputs can also reveal numerical variation that should be considered when checking later changes. Reusing the environment, local checkpoints, and compiler caches reduces the time spent on setup. When loading limits iteration, startup analysis measures loading phases and checkpoint page-cache residency so comparisons use comparable cache states. 

Find the bottleneck. The agent inspects the inference code and profiles the stages that appear expensive. Trace analysis helps locate costly modules, repeated operations, memory transfers, and synchronization. It then chooses an optimization that addresses the measured bottleneck using the techniques in Section IV-D. As tracing can substantially slow execution, latency is measured without the profiler. The inference timing tool synchronizes the GPU, retains the latency series, and checks whether the measurements have reached a steady level. 

Test the change. The agent measures the modified imple-mentation on the same GPU and compares its outputs with the saved baseline. Cache and CUDA-graph changes are also tested with changing inputs and resets. Changes that make inference slower, produce invalid outputs, or violate the required state or quality constraints are reverted. Unexpected numerical differences are investigated using the criteria in Section IV-C. A correct change with unchanged measured latency may be retained if it enables a specific follow-up optimization. Such changes are recorded as having no measured speedup. Approximate candidates can be explored before task evaluation is available; their acceptance remains pending until that evaluation is complete. 

Repeat and validate. After an improvement, the agent examines the remaining computation and decides the next experiment. This step matters because the benefit of an optimization depends on the changes already applied. Search continues within the time budget, with time reserved to remeasure the original policy and the best candidate under comparable conditions. The final evaluation checks numerical outputs, stateful behavior, and task performance as required by the type of change. Loading and compilation costs are recorded separately from warmed inference latency. Deadline analysis relates inference latency to the available replanning interval. For K actions executed between replans at control frequency f , this interval is K/f . This reports deployment headroom while keeping the workload fixed. External memory records validated notes from earlier runs, such as environment fixes and model-specific findings. The agent reuses these notes and can incorporate broader lessons into the optimization skill after a run. In Sec. V, when evaluating the harness itself, the guidance is held fixed, and both settings receive the same resources, search budget, and access to prior information. 

C. Correctness and Candidate Acceptance 

Different optimizations require different checks. WAMJET distinguishes three tiers:  

> •

Tier A: lossless acceleration. The change preserves the mathematical computation, precision, workload, and state behavior. The agent assesses eligibility through mathematical and code review and focused output and state checks. Equivalent compilation, fusion, and changes to GEMM or reduction order may introduce floating-point differences. Unexpected drift is investigated rather than rejected solely for exceeding an earlier numerical tolerance.  

> •

Tier B: validated approximation. The change approxi-mates internal computation while keeping the denoising steps, schedule, action horizon, and required outputs fixed. It must pass paired task evaluation within a specified bound on quality loss.  

> •

Tier C: changed workload or unvalidated approxi-mation. This includes fewer denoising steps, changed horizons or schedules, and approximations that lack passing task evaluation. The user sets the maximum acceptable tier. Invalid outputs, stale state, and known task-quality failures are rejected in all tiers. Lossless acceleration does not require bit-identical outputs. The numerical checker verifies output keys, shapes, dtypes, finite values, and input identity against the saved refer-ence. For each tensor, it reports the maximum absolute error and relative ℓ2 error. These measurements are assessed against the saved tolerances and, when available, the baseline’s run-to-run variation. The original reference is retained throughout the search so that successive small changes cannot hide cumulative drift. However, we find that the numerical drift checker could possibly make the acceleration too conservative, so in practice, the numerical checker is usually turned off. The agent tests stateful optimizations using input sequences such as A → B → A, comparing each output with its baseline reference under the same state history. It also verifies that resets restore the intended state. These checks help detect stale caches and incorrect reuse of buffer contents from earlier calls. Task performance is evaluated by running the baseline and candidate on matched episodes with identical initial states and random seeds. The number of episodes and acceptable performance loss are specified in advance, and final evaluation uses episodes excluded from calibration and optimization. WAMJET reports the difference in success rate and applies an exact McNemar test to the paired outcomes. Acceptance requires evidence that the candidate meets the predefined quality criterion. 

D. Acceleration Techniques 

The bottleneck determines which techniques the agent tries first. When inference repeatedly computes the same conditioning, prompt embeddings or K/V projections can be reused as long as their inputs remain unchanged. Unnecessary synchronization and distributed wrappers can be removed, while static buffers and CUDA graphs reduce allocation and dispatch overhead. These changes must preserve cache updates and ensure that graph replay receives the current inputs. When most time is spent in GPU kernels or memory transfers, the agent can try packed projections, faster imple-mentations of the same attention operation, different tensor layouts, or compiler specialization. Fusing normalization, modulation, activation, and residual operations can reduce intermediate memory traffic. Each change is evaluated through its effect on the complete inference call, since a faster kernel may have little effect on overall latency. When approximation is allowed, the search can also explore quantization and approximate reuse of features or K/V tensors. The agent verifies that the intended low-precision or reuse path executes and measures the resulting error. Conversion and cache-maintenance costs can offset the expected speedup, so the benefit must be measured for the actual workload. Useful candidates are then evaluated on robotic tasks. As earlier optimizations change the bottleneck, the agent can revisit techniques that initially gave little benefit. V. E XPERIMENTS

WAMJET supports hardware-aware optimization. The WAMJET-guided agent selects and refines quantization strate-gies through profiling and empirical search, adapting them to the target model and GPU architecture. On H100, both DreamZero and Cosmos3 use FP8 with per-output-channel weight scaling and per-token activation scaling. On B200, DreamZero uses a mixed-precision configuration: NVFP4 for the feed-forward projections and FP8 for the remaining linear projections within the diffusion-transformer blocks. Cosmos3 instead uses MXFP8 for its generation projections. The agent combines these quantization strategies with lossless optimizations, including compilation and scheduling, and reassesses the remaining bottlenecks after each change.

E. Optimization Progress over Search Time 

We plot the optimization progress of GPT-6-astra on DreamZero and OpenWAM using B200. Fig. 3 shows individ-ual trials and accepted optimization trajectories, illustrating how performance improves through iterative exploration. With WAMJET, the coding agent autonomously profiles each candidate, identifies new bottlenecks, and iteratively explores optimization opportunities. In contrast, without our harness, coding agents tend to be overly conservative and terminate early, leaving substantial optimization opportunities unexplored. Furthermore, Fig. 3 also annotates representative points with the key optimizations introduced during the search. Although DreamZero and OpenWAM share techniques such as CUDA Graphs and operator fusion, their final optimization recipes differ even on the same GPU. The results indicate that the guided agent adopts adaptive, model-specific optimization strategies according to each model’s bottlenecks. 

F. Startup Cost Acceleration 

WAMJET also guides the coding agent in optimizing model startup cost. In recorded B200 runs, DreamZero’s startup time decreases from 208 .1 to 30 .3 s, and OpenWAM’s decreases from 79 .6 to 8.2 s. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented WAMJET, a harness for accelerating WAM inference through iterative code optimization and evaluation. WAMJET guides a coding agent to find bottlenecks, test acceleration techniques, and check their effects on the policy. Experiments across six models show improvements from conditioning reuse, reduced execution overhead, and kernel optimization, with additional gains from continued search. Quantization can further reduce latency, but its effect on task performance requires evaluation. Future work will focus on broader paired task tests and controlled comparisons of the harness under matched search budgets.
