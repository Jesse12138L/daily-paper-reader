Abstract
World Action Models (WAMs) emerge as a promising foundation for policy refinement by
predicting the consequences of sampled actions. However, visually plausible predictions can
mislead policy refinement if they fail to reflect the input actions. To address this mismatch, we
introduce R2-WAM, a two-stage repair-and-reject post-training framework that first improves
the consistency of predicted futures with input actions, then uses these futures to select infe-
rior action samples for negative fine-tuning. The repair stage grounds imagination in observed
robot behavior through a kinematic alignment score that measures agreement between predicted
and demonstrated motion, enabling the predicted video to faithfully reflect its input actions. Us-
ing the repaired video model, the rejection stage compares imagined outcomes of sampled and
demonstrated actions, selectively applying negative fine-tuning to samples whose predicted task
progress falls below the demonstrated reference by a prescribed margin. Together, the two stages
extend video prediction from representation learning to consequence-based policy refinement
without additional environment interaction or changes to the inference procedure. R2-WAM
achieves 93.8% average success on RoboTwin 2.0 across clean and randomized settings. On the
long-horizon real-world Fold Shirt task, it achieves 87.5% average success, compared with 0%
for Fast-WAM.

Introduction
World Action Models (WAMs) have recently emerged as a promising approach to robotic control
by guiding action generation with predicted visual outcomes. Existing WAMs typically learn to
predict video and actions jointly in pixel or latent space (Ye et al., 2026b; Zhu et al., 2025; Bi
et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026), thereby grounding action generation in predicted
scene dynamics. Beyond direct robot control, recent studies have explored using action-conditioned
world models as simulators to predict the consequences of candidate actions and provide feedback
for policy improvement (Guo et al., 2026b; Yang et al., 2026a). This is particularly useful when
demonstrations provide limited coverage of execution errors and collecting physical trials is costly
(Chandra et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2026).
A key prerequisite for effective policy post-training with a world model is that it faithfully pre-
dicts how the environment changes in response to actions. Although modern video generation mod-
els can produce visually plausible predictions (Wan et al., 2025), they do not necessarily preserve
physical properties such as kinematics and dynamics. Consequently, generated videos may appear
realistic while failing to reflect the supplied actions (Yang et al., 2026b). Evaluating actions with
such predictions can mistake video model errors for policy failures, causing the policy to learn from
outcomes its actions would not produce(Jiang et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026).

To reduce mismatch between imagined and actual consequences, prior methods ground gen-
erated videos in robot motion using inverse dynamics (Wang et al., 2026) or extracted 3D flow
(Dharmarajan et al., 2025). These methods rely on auxiliary components to obtain motion feedback,
but do not ensure that the video faithfully follows a supplied action. Recent methods improve imag-
ined feedback by fine-tuning world models on real policy rollouts (Guo et al., 2026a), updating the
model as the policy evolves from task-critical keyframes (Jiang et al., 2026), or training on diverse
actions with supervision (Chen et al., 2026). These methods improve action grounding, but leave
open how observed demonstration outcomes can identify unreliable predictions before they are used
to evaluate sampled policy actions.
To fill this gap, we propose repairing the video expert using observed consequences of demon-
strated actions, then using the repaired expert to reject sampled actions with inferior predicted task
progress (Figure 1). To this end, we introduce R2-WAM, a two-stage repair-and-reject post-training
framework comprising predictive repair and action rejection (Figure 2), comprising the following
modules:
1) Predictive repair turns each demonstration into a test of action fidelity: given the same ob-
servation and action, the video expert generates a future that can be compared with what the robot
actually did. A kinematic alignment score measures whether the generated robot motion follows
the observed motion and selects predictions with substantial discrepancies for negative fine-tuning.
This stage improves the video expert before its imagined consequences are used to judge sampled
policy actions, reducing the risk that prediction errors become misleading training signals.
2) Action rejection uses the repaired video expert to judge what sampled actions are likely to
achieve. With the expert frozen, we predict futures for sampled and demonstrated actions from the
same initial observation, then compare their task progress using STEAM (Liu et al., 2026). Sampled
actions whose predicted progress falls below the demonstrated reference by a prescribed margin
are selected for negative fine-tuning, while demonstrations remain the positive targets. This directs
policy updates toward actions with evidence of inferior consequences.
Together, the two stages turn video prediction into feedback for policy post-training without ad-
ditional environment interaction or changes to the inference procedure. We conduct an extensive
empirical study across 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks and two real-world manipulation tasks, evaluating
action fidelity, negative-selection accuracy, and downstream policy success. R2-WAM improves av-
erage success over action-conditioned Fast-WAM by 3.3 percentage points in simulation and raises
average success on the long-horizon real-world Fold Shirt task from 0% with Fast-WAM to 87.5%.

Related Work
World action models.
World action models couple future prediction with action generation through
joint diffusion modeling of video and actions (Ye et al., 2026b; Zhu et al., 2025), causal modeling (Li
et al., 2026), and latent action representations (Bi et al., 2026). WorldScape Policy 2.0 (Su et al.,
2026) extends this framework with memory and multimodal conditioning. Predictions can also guide
control through visual representations (Hu et al., 2024) or explicit motion plans: RoboFlow4D (Lin
et al., 2026a) predicts multi-frame 3D motion to condition action generation. Fast-WAM (Yuan
et al., 2026) couples video and action experts through shared attention, using video prediction to
learn world representations without generating future videos at deployment. We build on Fast-WAM
to use predicted consequences for policy post-training.
Policy post-training.
RECAP (Intelligence et al., 2025a) refines policies through advantage-conditioned
learning from experience. World-model-based approaches use synthetic rollouts and predicted con-
sequences for policy fine-tuning (Guo et al., 2026b), advantage estimation (Yang et al., 2026a),
and preference optimization (Hung et al., 2025). To improve the reliability of imagined feedback,
VLAW (Guo et al., 2026a) adapts its world model using real rollouts, while WoVR (Jiang et al.,
2026) regulates imagined rollouts and co-evolves the world model and policy. Our approach uses
observed demonstration futures to repair action-inconsistent predictions without additional environ-

video generation at inference, R2-WAM adopts the shared attention world action model architecture
of Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026). Each expert demonstration comprises xi = (oi, si, ℓi), consisting

The architecture comprises a video expert and an action expert, both implemented as diffusion
transformers. The video stream contains the latent of the current frame and noised future video
latents, whereas the action stream contains noised action chunks. Within each MoT block, the
two streams interact through shared masked self-attention, followed by cross-attention to state and
language representations. In our configuration, the video expert also receives the action chunk. The
video expert defines the action-conditioned video model Wϕ(y | x, a), and the action expert defines
πθ(a | x). The video expert therefore supplies world representations for action generation and maps
candidate actions to imagined consequences during post-training.

Consequence-Grounded Post-Training
R2-WAM refines a world action model through predictive repair followed by action rejection (Fig-
ure 2). Predictive repair uses observed futures to identify and suppress action-inconsistent video
predictions (Section 4.1). With the repaired video expert frozen, action rejection compares the
imagined progress of sampled and demonstrated actions to select inferior action samples for nega-
tive fine-tuning (Section 4.2).

Experiments
Our experiments address five questions. (Q1) Simulation performance: how does R2-WAM com-
pare with recent vision language action policies and world action models on RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen
et al., 2025)? (Q2) Negative selection: does predictive repair improve the agreement between nega-
tive selections based on imagined consequences and STEAM judgments of actual executions? (Q3)
Ablation studies: which post-training components and action training objectives account for the
gains in task success? (Q4) Real-world effectiveness: does R2-WAM improve task completion
in long-horizon real-world manipulation? (Q5) Behavioral analysis: do imagined consequences
reveal the lack of progress observed during shirt folding?
5.1
Simulation on RoboTwin 2.0
Experimental protocol.
We follow the RoboTwin 2.0 multitask protocol used by Fast-WAM. The
training set combines 2,500 demonstrations collected in clean scenes and 25,000 demonstrations
collected with scene randomization, spanning 50 bimanual manipulation tasks. We evaluate every
task in both clean and randomized environments using 100 closed-loop trials per setting, and report
success rates averaged over the 50 tasks.
Our base model is Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026) with an action-conditioned video expert. We
first train both experts jointly with flow matching for 5 epochs. Action-conditioned Fast-WAM
continues joint flow matching for 2 additional epochs, whereas R2-WAM applies predictive repair
followed by action post-training, training each expert for 2 epochs. Appendix E.1 details the ablation
protocol, and Appendix C reports the optimization and sampling settings.
Main comparison.
To answer Q1, we compare R2-WAM with recent vision-language action poli-
cies and world action models (Table 1). R2-WAM achieves the highest success rates in both eval-
uation settings, reaching 93.8% in clean environments and 93.7% under scene randomization. The
gains over LingBot-VA in clean environments and HoloBrain-0-QW under randomization are 0.9

5.2
Action Negative Selection
To answer Q2, we test whether predictive repair improves the selection of action negatives from
imagined consequences on evaluation episodes. Across 500 contexts from all 50 RoboTwin tasks,
we predict consequences for 2,000 shared candidate actions using video experts trained with con-
tinued flow matching or predictive repair. Each candidate and its expert reference are also executed
from the same simulator state. The same frozen STEAM evaluator scores actual and imagined con-
sequences, providing reference labels from actual executions and predicted selections from imagined
futures. These simulator executions are used only for evaluation, not for post-training. We evalu-
ate margins of 0.02, 0.05, and 0.08, applying the same margin to actual and imagined consequence
gaps in each comparison. Precision and recall are computed from pooled counts across the 2,000
candidates. Predictive repair improves negative-selection precision and recall at all three margins
(Table 2). At the training margin of 0.08, precision increases from 64.6% to 69.8%, while recall rises
from 35.5% to 50.6%. With candidate actions and the STEAM evaluator held fixed, the improved
agreement with execution-based judgments suggests that predictive repair provides more reliable
predictions for selecting action negatives. Appendix E.3 provides the protocol and complete counts.

5.4
Real-World Evaluation
Robot setup and tasks.
We evaluate on a PIPER platform with two arms, equipped with one front
camera and two RGB cameras mounted on the wrists. The policy receives the three camera views
and the current robot state, and predicts bimanual end-effector action chunks. We evaluate two
long-horizon tasks covering deformable manipulation and tabletop cleanup. In Fold Shirt, the robot
transforms a shirt into a rectangular configuration through an extended sequence of coordinated
bimanual actions. In Clean Table, it repeatedly selects, grasps, and places bottles and bottle caps
from the tabletop into a trash bin. Hardware, data, and deployment details appear in Appendix F.1.
Baselines.
We compare against 4 baselines: π0.5 (Intelligence et al., 2025b), X-VLA (Zheng et al.,
2026a), Motus (Bi et al., 2026), and Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026). Each baseline is trained for 7
epochs on the same demonstrations for each task, using its original architecture, observation pro-
cessing, and action representation. We initialize R2-WAM from Fast-WAM after 5 epochs and train
each expert for 2 additional epochs using its corresponding post-training objective.
Evaluation protocol.
We organize evaluation as a 2×2 design over initial position and object
instance. Seen uses the training position region and familiar objects. Unseen Position and Un-
seen Object vary one factor at a time, while Unseen Position + Object varies both. Each condition
contains 20 autonomous trials per method and task. Each trained policy is evaluated across all 4 con-
ditions. Figure 3(a–b) reports success for each task; complete numerical results, Wilson confidence
intervals, and the complete protocol are provided in Appendices F.3 and F.4.
Real-world performance.
To answer Q4, we compare task success on Fold Shirt and Clean Table
(Figure 3(a–b)). On Fold Shirt, a long-horizon bimanual task, R2-WAM achieves 87.5% average
success, whereas neither Fast-WAM nor Motus completes the task in any evaluation trial. The VLA
baselines, π0.5 and X-VLA, achieve higher success than these WAM baselines but remain below
R2-WAM across all four evaluation conditions. On Clean Table, R2-WAM also outperforms all
evaluated baselines across the four conditions. Under joint variation, it achieves 70.0% success,
exceeding the strongest baseline, π0.5, by 30 percentage points.
Qualitative analysis of shirt folding.
To answer Q5, we compare Fast-WAM executions with
imagined consequences at three stages of shirt folding (Figure 3(c)). At each stage, Fast-WAM es-
tablishes a grasp or contact but does not complete the subsequent garment motion. During bimanual
folding, it lifts the garment without folding it forward. During repositioning, it contacts the gar-
ment without pulling it toward the robot. During lateral folding, it holds and lifts a corner without

Conclusion
We presented R2-WAM, a repair-and-reject framework for post-training world action models. Pre-
dictive repair uses observed demonstration futures to improve the action fidelity of video predic-
tions. The repaired video expert then guides negative fine-tuning of sampled actions whose predicted
progress falls below the demonstrated reference. Experiments on RoboTwin 2.0 and two real-world
manipulation tasks show improved task completion, including long-horizon shirt folding that Fast-
WAM fails to complete. Further analysis shows that predictive repair improves negative-selection
accuracy, while action rejection yields larger gains in task success with the repaired video expert.

In simulation, training uses demonstrations from all 50 RoboTwin tasks, comprising 2,500 clean
and 25,000 randomized episodes. For real-world evaluation, we train separate scorers for Fold Shirt
and Clean Table using the same task demonstrations as policy training (Table 9). Frame pairs are
sampled with a maximum temporal separation of k frames: k = 32 for RoboTwin and Fold Shirt,
and k = 64 for Clean Table. The classifier retains 32 temporal bins in all settings.

predictor is trained for 16,000 steps using AdamW with learning rate 5 × 10−5, weight decay 10−5,

shared by the encoders and prediction head. Following STEAM, we train an ensemble of three
predictors and take the minimum of their progress estimates as ASTEAM. Positive scores indicate
progress and negative scores indicate regression. The ensemble remains frozen during WAM post-

future.

Experimental setup.
We compare five objectives for post-training the video expert. Continued
flow matching serves as a control with the same training data and number of optimizer updates,
but uses only the observed futures. Repair with PSNR compares generated and observed head-

below 26 dB are selected as negatives and assigned weight one in the negative fine-tuning objective.

each geometric component to predictive repair.
All repair variants share the initialization, training data, sampling budget, and negative fine-

Results.
Repair with PSNR reduces the action-following failure rate from 32.0% to 18.6%, in-
dicating that generic visual feedback corrects some action-following errors. The complete action-
following score further reduces the failure rate to 6.8% and increases tracking success from 93.0%

feedback corrects prediction errors that are not captured by visual similarity, without trading off
standard video fidelity.
Among the individual geometric components, relative-trajectory consistency provides the stronger
action-following signal, yielding a failure rate of 10.6%, compared with 17.0% for absolute-position
consistency. Combining both components further reduces the failure rate to 6.8% and achieves the
highest trackability, PSNR, and SSIM. The two terms are therefore complementary: relative trajecto-
ries characterize the execution of the conditioning action, while absolute positions retain information
about kinematic alignment.

Evaluation protocol.
We compare continued flow matching and predictive repair in the clean
RoboTwin setting across all 50 tasks. For each task, 10 independently generated successful scripted-
expert trajectories provide one context each, giving 500 contexts. The decision point is placed at
60% of the valid 32-step action-chunk start range. The next 32 expert actions form the reference
continuation; completion of the entire task within this horizon is not required.
For each context, four candidate action chunks are sampled once from a fixed policy and reused
for both video models. Each candidate and the expert continuation are executed from the same
restored simulator state, producing 2,000 candidate executions and 500 reference executions. Each
video model also predicts futures under the expert continuation and the identical candidate actions.
This diagnostic uses simulator executions to evaluate the consequence comparisons, rather than as
supervision for the post-training objective.

Real-World Evaluation Details
F.1
Robot Platform and Policy Deployment
We use a PIPER platform with two arms (Figure 4). Each arm has six actuated joints and a gripper
with one degree of freedom. Visual observations are provided by three RGB cameras: one front
camera and one camera mounted on each wrist. The raw camera streams are recorded at 480×640
resolution and 30 Hz. At inference, the front view is resized to 320×256, the two wrist views are
resized to 160×128, and the views are assembled into a 384×320 combined camera input (height ×
width).
The policy predicts a 20D absolute bimanual end-effector action, comprising position, a 6D
rotation representation, and gripper state for each arm. The policy uses 10 Euler steps to generate a
32-step action chunk at 30 Hz, with an execution horizon of 24 control steps.

F.5
Component Ablations on Fold Shirt
We evaluate predictive repair and action rejection separately on Fold Shirt (Table 12). Each variant
is evaluated over 20 autonomous trials in each of the four conditions described in Appendix F.3,
totaling 80 trials. Repair / GT FM applies predictive repair to the video expert while training the
action expert with demonstration flow matching. GT FM / Rejection trains the video expert with
demonstration flow matching and uses its imagined consequences for action rejection. Neither
single-stage variant completes the task in these trials. Predictive repair alone does not yield suc-
cessful task completion when the action expert is trained solely with demonstration flow matching.
Conversely, action rejection without predictive repair also remains insufficient. Combining the two
stages achieves 87.5% average success, supporting their complementary roles in long-horizon shirt
folding: improving the action fidelity of predicted consequences and using that feedback to refine
sampled actions.
