ABSTRACT
A robot demonstration records two things in the same frames: what happened to
the objects, and how one particular arm made it happen. We condition on the first.
A demonstration is compiled into an effect program: the 3D keypoint trajectories
of the objects that moved, two points marking where each was held, and the con-
figuration the scene ends in, with the demonstrator removed. PEWAM, a 71.5M-
parameter world-action model, generates effect, robot execution, action and ter-
minal state as four streams with independent flow-matching times, so clamping a
program and sampling the execution turns inference into programming, re-solved
closed loop from the live scene. On held-out LIBERO-Goal tasks, one demon-
stration’s program completes 40 of 90 episodes, where the same backbone given a
goal image or language, and published demonstration-conditioned methods, com-
plete at most 19; on three of Meta-World’s held-out classes it exceeds the best
published results, though not on the five-class mean. Because a program is a set of
coordinates, a person can edit it: the placement follows a shifted terminal state and
the grasp turns with rotated contact points. The same program runs on four robot
arms without retraining, and after a push, re-solving completes 23 of 60 episodes
where replaying the demonstration completes 6. On a Franka arm, fine-tuned on
real demonstrations of other tasks, programs compiled from single human videos
complete 36 of 40 trials, against 22 for the same backbone conditioned on the
video’s last frame as a goal image.

INTRODUCTION
Showing a robot what to do is often easier than describing it, and a single demonstration is a direct
way to specify a new manipulation task (Vosylius & Johns, 2025; Di Palo & Johns, 2024; Goswami
et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). A demonstration, however, records two things in the same frames:
what happened to the objects, and how one particular arm made it happen. Watch someone put a
bowl on a stove and you learn where the bowl should end up, roughly how it travels and where it
can be held; you do not learn to bend your elbow the way they bent theirs. Most systems that learn
from demonstrations keep the two together. Policies that transfer the demonstrator’s motion keep
the body and treat the world as context (Park et al., 2026; Wen et al., 2024; Haldar & Pinto, 2025),
and video-action models prompted with a demonstration predict both at once, as one pixel-level
future (Li et al., 2026b; Zhou et al., 2026). The task is then entangled with one body’s motion: no
variable states it on its own, so it can be neither edited nor given to another robot.
This viewpoint exposes a limitation of both dominant formulations. Conditioning on the demonstra-
tor’s trajectory or video (Zhu et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2024; Vosylius & Johns, 2025) ties the task to
one execution, which is valid only for the arm, the grasp and the layout that produced it. Methods
built on object motion do separate the object from the actor (Xu et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024;
Bharadhwaj et al., 2024; Dharmarajan et al., 2026), but they generate that motion from language
or video and execute it with a planner, a controller or a per-task policy. What is missing is a task

As illustrated in Figure 1, our key observation is that the change in the world is the task, and the
demonstrator’s motion is one of many ways to produce it. We therefore compile a demonstration into
an effect program: keypoints of each relevant object tracked over time, two contact points recording
where the object was held, and the configuration the objects end in. Nothing in the program refers
to the robot. Based on this view we propose PEWAM, a world-action model that describes effect,
end-effector execution, native action and terminal state as four streams with separate flow-matching
times (Bao et al., 2023; Lipman et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2025), so that every conditional is a query
of the same weights. Clamping the program and sampling the execution poses the question of how
to make the effect happen, which turns inference into programming. At deployment the unfinished
part of the program is re-solved from the live scene every few control steps. Because a push moves
an object just as a grasp does, progress through the program is counted only for effects the robot has
caused, and a disturbance becomes a new observation.
We evaluate PEWAM on LIBERO, Meta-World and RLBench, on four arms, under pushes, from
pixels and on a real Franka arm programmed by human videos. Where the demonstrator’s motion
stays valid, copying it is stronger; the program pays off where copying breaks: after a push, on
another arm, and from a person’s hand.
We summarize our contributions as follows: (i) we formulate the task carried by a demonstration
as an actor-free effect program of object keypoints, contact points and a terminal state, and propose
PEWAM, a world-action model whose independently noised streams let one set of weights both
execute and predict; (ii) we show that the program is an interface a person can edit and hand to
another body: its terminal state moves the placement, its contact points turn the grasp, and one
program runs on four simulated arms and, from a human video, on a real arm; (iii) we treat progress
as an effect the robot causes and gate the program on engagement, so that re-solving absorbs pushes
that defeat replay; and (iv) we show that editability needs contact written as points, not weights, and
training data with several executions of one effect.

RELATED WORK
2.1
WORLD-ACTION MODELS AND IMITATION FROM ONE DEMONSTRATION
World-action models learn video and action jointly, so that one network can both predict
and act.
UWM (Zhu et al., 2025) couples video and action diffusion with independent
timesteps, JOPAT (Guan et al., 2026) adds a point-track stream, and Hydra-0 and Zero-WAM
2


(Li et al., 2026b; Zhou et al., 2026) condition multi-billion-parameter video models on flow or on
a human video; generalist policies and pretrained world-action models specify the task with a sen-
tence (Kim et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026). Methods that imitate one demonstration at test time
read it as point-cloud graphs (Vosylius & Johns, 2025), as keypoint and action tokens (Di Palo &
Johns, 2024), through demonstration-to-scene correspondences (She et al., 2026) or as the video
itself (Goswami et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025), or they retarget the demonstrator’s trajectory (Park
et al., 2026); each carries the demonstrator’s motion. We keep independent timesteps but condition
on an actor-free program that a person can read and edit.
2.2
OBJECT MOTION AS THE TASK INTERFACE
Several methods drop the actor and pass the object’s motion to the robot. Most generate that motion,
as 2D or 3D flow from language, images, point clouds or generated video (Xu et al., 2024; Bharad-
hwaj et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a; Dharmarajan
et al., 2026) or as a trajectory from a per-task model (Hsu et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2025), and exe-
cute it with a flow-conditioned policy, a controller or trajectory optimisation. Others reproduce the
motion in one human video by optimisation, retargeting or a policy trained for that demonstration
(Zhu et al., 2026; Kerr et al., 2024; Lum et al., 2025). Keypoints and trajectories also serve as task
specifications (Manuelli et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2020; Gu et al., 2024), ATM
and Point Policy (Wen et al., 2024; Haldar & Pinto, 2025) condition on point tracks (Karaev et al.,
2024) that include the actor’s motion, and demonstration editing adapts recorded demonstrations to
new layouts to synthesise data (Mandlekar et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2025). We share the premise that
the object’s motion is the task. Closest to us, Im2Flow2Act (Xu et al., 2024) also carries a task from
human video to a robot through object flow, but generates the flow with a separate model and exe-
cutes it with a flow-conditioned policy; we read the effect from the demonstration itself, add contact
points and a terminal state, and sample the execution from one multi-task world-action model that
also answers forward queries of the same program.

Training data.
The model trains on LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) tasks outside the held-out set. A
paired multi-solution corpus adds executions that realise one effect through grasps rotated about the
object’s vertical axis; solutions of one effect differ by a median 59 mm in execution and 1.0 mm in
terminal state. Every solution is conditioned on the program its group shares, so the contact points
are the only input that tells the solutions of one effect apart.

EXPERIMENTS
Benchmarks and protocol.
We evaluate on held-out tasks and objects of LIBERO, on the test
classes of Meta-World ML10 and ML45, on RLBench, on four robot arms and on a real Franka arm
programmed by human video (Table 6); simulated rollouts use the benchmark’s own success check.
LIBERO episodes re-plan every Kexec=4 steps under a 400-step cap, with one fixed environment and
sampling seed and 30 episodes per task and cell. As for the published baselines, a LIBERO episode
starts where the demonstration that programs it started; on Meta-World each episode is programmed
by another episode’s demonstration. Outside §4.4, keypoints come from simulator state. Counts are
from one training; replicates move the LIBERO held-out sum by up to seven (Appendix D).
4.1
ONE DEMONSTRATION PROGRAMS A HELD-OUT TASK

LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSION
Effects that barely move an object, such as turning a knob, leave the program little to specify; the
joint-position channel of Meta-World is the natural extension (Appendix C). The pointer and gate
are hand-set rules for grasped objects; entities are rigid boxes, mostly read from simulator state;
9


where the demonstrator’s motion stays valid, copying it is stronger; RLBench’s specialist remains
far ahead; and most cells are single trainings of 30 episodes, whose replicates can move by as much
as the differences we discuss.
Keeping only what a demonstration did to the world gives a world-action model an input that people
can edit and that carries across bodies, and reading progress from caused effects lets it absorb a push.

The closed-loop experiments use the public LIBERO, RoboCasa, Meta-World and RLBench bench-

A
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Inputs and queries.
Each observation contains two 1282 RGB views (the agent view and the
wrist camera), 16-D proprioception (end-effector position, orientation and gripper state, plus the
arm’s seven joint angles), and the entities’ current keypoints, which serve as an anchor. During
training each example is posed as one of four queries, drawn with probabilities 0.55 (inverse), 0.25
(goal), 0.15 (forward) and 0.05 (inverse dynamics), and conditions are dropped with probability 0.1.
Model and training.
All streams are processed by one shared DiT backbone with depth 8, width
512, 8 heads and 8 register tokens (71.5M parameters). A per-view ResNet-18 with GroupNorm
encodes the observation, and the entity keypoints at t=0 enter as an anchor token. The effect is
written in absolute normalised world coordinates, and the stream losses are weighted E:R:A:G =
1:1:1:0.5. The inverse query gets extra regularisation: G is given clean with probability 0.8, the
condition dropout of 0.1 applies here too, and images are randomly cropped by 4 pixels. We use
batch 64 per rank on three ranks and AdamW (learning rate 3×10−4, weight decay 10−4, ϵ=10−6),
with 1000 warm-up steps, gradient clipping at 1.0 and EMA 0.999. Training runs in fp32 because
bf16 autocast diverged twice between 32k and 33k steps. The learning rate follows a 60k cosine
schedule that we stop at 30k, a point the longest run, from 20k to 60k, supports with bowl-task sums
over the four robot arms of 81, 74, 73, 76 and 66. One training run takes about three and a half hours
on three RTX 3090s (195 and 221 minutes for the two replicates we timed).
Training data.
The LIBERO data covers 107 tasks (LIBERO-90, LIBERO-10 and the LIBERO-
Goal tasks outside the held-out set), which yield 1.16M training windows. The paired multi-solution
corpus adds 56 scenes with 273 executions.

Sampling.
We sample with 20 Euler steps and keep clamped tokens clean throughout. The control
loop re-plans every four control steps by calling the inverse query, so the cost of one re-plan sets the
loop’s speed. On one RTX A6000, excluding simulation, a re-plan takes a median of 348 ms with
20 steps and 117 ms with 5 (measured over 125 and 96 re-plans in two bowl episodes).
The paired multi-solution corpus.
The corpus gives the model several executions of the same
effect. To build it, we rotate the demonstrated grasp about the object’s own vertical axis, which
moves the wrist but not the object, and replay each rotated grasp in the simulator. The replays do not
reproduce the object motion exactly, so conditioning each execution on its own program would let
small program differences stand in for the choice of grasp. We therefore condition every execution
in a group on the group’s canonical program. This shrinks the difference between programs of one
group from 28.3 mm to 0.47 mm (after phase alignment) and from 12.5 mm to zero at the terminal
state, while the contact points still differ by 39.9 mm. Within a group, the contact points are thus the
only part of the program that tells the executions apart. Across the two shards the headline model
trains on (56 scenes, 273 executions), executions of one effect differ by a median of 59 mm and end
in terminal states that differ by 1.0 mm.

PRE-REGISTERED CRITERIA AND OUTCOMES
For every architectural variant, the reading of its results was fixed in writing before any of its num-
bers were seen. Each criteria file is under version control with the commit that introduced it and the
date of that commit, and that history will be released with the code upon acceptance. For example,
the criteria for v13 were committed on 09-07 at 07:58, and the run they judge was configured on
09-08 at 01:38; for v14 the criteria were committed on 09-09 at 22:13 and the run was configured
on 09-10 at 02:22. The criteria reported in this paper and their outcomes are:
• Contact as affine weights (v14). Criteria: mean following over ±20◦, ±45◦of at least
0.85, a held-out effect-program sum of at least 40 of 90, a −45◦success of at least 24 of 30
and a cross-arm bowl sum of at least 81. Measured: a held-out sum of 44, a cross-arm bowl
sum of 85, a −45◦success of 13, and a realised offset of the same sign at every commanded
rotation, the drift of §4.5. The paper keeps contact as two points.
• Dense surface support (v13). Pass marks: following 0.85, held-out sum 40, −45◦success
24. Measured: 0.31, 29 and 14. The paper keeps two contact points.
• Axis-symmetric corpus (v15), gated before training. Hypothesis: the direction asymme-
try of contact following reflects how often each orientation occurs in the corpus. Gate: the
density at the less-followed orientation is at most half the density at the other. The mea-
sured ratio is 1.10, so the corpus stays as it is. Measurements also cleared a modulo-180
read-out artefact, a yaw asymmetry in the corpus, the support band of training orientations
and wrist joint limits; two further trainings show that the training seed sets the direction of
the asymmetry (Appendix F).
• Training past 30k steps (W8, 20k to 60k). At least one checkpoint had to reach a four-
arm bowl sum of 76; the three checkpoints the rule judges give 73, 76 and 66. The 50k
checkpoint reaches 76, and the headline model stays at 30k, where every experiment of the
paper runs.

C
SCOPE
On LIBERO, the held-out tasks are new goals in known scenes and new objects for a known skill.
Effects that are small rotations of a fixed object, such as a stove knob, a lever or a drawer handle, give
the program little motion to specify; the joint-position channel for articulated entities that PEWAM
uses on Meta-World ML45 extends the program to such effects. RoboCasa changes the kitchen and
the mobile base every episode (Appendix K). On RLBench the program adds 52 successes to the
same recipe without a program (Appendix M).

Replicate trainings.
Retraining with a new seed moves the held-out sum by up to seven episodes
for the effect program (40, 38, 33) and by up to 18 for the terminal state (49, 50, 32), and a four-arm
bowl sum by up to 22 (Appendix N). Differences are read against the replicate spread of the statistic
they concern.
Layout variation.
Over the 110 tasks in the corpus, the manipulated object’s starting position
differs between two episodes of a task by a median 2.5 cm, and in 99% of tasks it stays under 3 cm
(bowl task: median 1.7 cm). Layout transfer is measured on Meta-World, where every episode is
programmed by another episode’s demonstration (§4.1).
Held-out tasks have near neighbours, some nearer than others.
How new a held-out task is
depends on how far its effect lies from the effects seen in training. We take a task’s effect to be
the median displacement of the entity that moves. The closest training task to bowl→stove is then
4.2 cm away, compared with 25 cm of bowl travel, and the closest to bottle→cabinet is 10.7 cm
away, compared with 33. The knob moves 0.3 cm, and two training tasks have effects that match
it to one decimal place. The knob is also turned in three other LIBERO-90 scenes, where the main
checkpoint completes it from its program 10 of 10 times; in the held-out scene it completes 1 of 30.
Success is nearly binary on pick-and-place.
On the bowl and bottle tasks, failures are 400-step
timeouts that leave the object a median 26 and 33 cm from its programmed destination, with the
object still at its start. Successes end 2.1 and 1.6 cm away, and no episode falls in between. The
knob behaves differently: failures stop a median 4.8 cm from the target, against 2.0 cm for a success.
What the seed changes.
To see where the spread between trainings comes from, we compare
the three trainings episode by episode. On the bowl task they agree on all 30 episodes under the
terminal-state query and on 26 of 30 under the full program (24 succeed under every seed, 2 under
none). On the bottle they agree on 13: three episodes succeed under all three trainings, ten fail under
all three, and seventeen change with the seed. A failure looks the same under every seed, a timeout
with the bottle 33 to 34 cm from its destination. The seed therefore decides which episodes get the
object picked up, and each outcome keeps its form across seeds.
Attributing editability to the corpus.
We compare how closely PEWAM follows a commanded
rotation of the contact points with how closely W8 does, the variant that has the contact channel but
not the corpus. The test was fixed before the last two trainings: one further training of each variant
(seed 5, because on two GPUs every lower seed reuses a per-rank seed of an earlier training), 50
episodes per commanded rotation for all seven trainings on one machine, and an exact one-sided
permutation test over four PEWAM and three W8 trainings at p ≤0.05, which requires every
PEWAM training to follow more closely than every W8 training. The two new trainings follow at
0.66 and 0.34; all twelve pairs order the same way, giving p=1/35=0.029.

Test-time changes.
These changes alter how the trained main model is queried, without retraining
it. Collapsing the two contact points to their midpoint gives four-arm sums of 77 and 40, against
89 and 43. Feeding each arm its own joint angles, instead of re-expressing proprioception in the

more than ten: advancing the program window by the demonstration’s clock, which gives 27, with
the bottle at 1 of 30.
Retrained variants.
Each of these variants retrains the main recipe with one design choice
changed. Training the inverse query alone (R1) completes 41 of 90 and follows the two ±45◦
commanded rotations at 0.83 and 0.93, against 0.47 and 0.82 for the main model. Sharing one

PUBLISHED BASELINES ON THE HELD-OUT PROTOCOL
This appendix describes how each published method was run on our held-out protocol. All baselines
use the same episodes and the same success test: the first 30 held-out demonstrations of each task,
each episode reset to its demonstration’s initial state, a limit of 400 control steps, and the BDDL
success check.
H.1
UWM AND THE DEMONSTRATOR’S OWN TRAJECTORY
UWM can be conditioned on future frames, so it reads a demonstration directly. We query the
released checkpoint through its own sample inverse dynamics, giving it the demonstration’s
future frames as the target and advancing that target as the execution proceeds; we run it both
at its own chunk length and at ours. Two controls show what its row means. The first checks
our evaluation loop: on two tasks UWM was trained on, it succeeds 18 of 20 times. The second
concerns what UWM has seen. Its pretraining corpus, LIBERO-90, contains two tasks whose effect
is essentially the knob’s, and the knob is the one task where it does well, with 19 of 30 (12 at our
chunk length). It saw neither LIBERO-10 nor our seven LIBERO-Goal training tasks, which is why
the other tasks stay out of reach. For reference, under the same setup our headline weights complete
28, 28 and 29 of 30, and replaying the demonstration’s recorded actions completes 29, 28 and 30.
If the demonstrator is the same robot, the simplest way to use a demonstration is to copy its trajectory,
so we also measure how well that works. We use the earlier backbone with re-planning every eight
steps and take the demonstration from a different episode of the same task. On the bowl and the

H.2
INSTANT POLICY
We run the released checkpoint unchanged and adapt only its input and output to LIBERO. Input:
Instant Policy reads point clouds, which we build from the depth and element segmentation of three
static LIBERO cameras. Following the convention of its RLBench evaluation, we keep every geom
that belongs to neither the robot nor the table, back-project it to the world frame, and crop it to the
table’s footprint and the space above it. The resulting cloud of each object lies a median 4.4 cm
from the object’s centre in the simulator. The demonstration given in context is the episode’s own,
replayed state by state and observed with the same cameras. Output: each predicted end-effector
pose is reached through LIBERO’s operational-space controller in one to four control steps, with a
median residual of 4 mm and 0.2◦. Because these steps count against the same 400-step limit, an
episode allows only about fifteen queries. Tripling the step budget and giving two demonstrations
yields 0, 0 and 8 of 30, against 0, 0 and 7 at 400 steps.
H.3
ATM
No LIBERO checkpoint of ATM covers our tasks, so we train it with its released code on the 90
LIBERO-90 tasks and the seven LIBERO-Goal training tasks, with 45 training and 5 validation
demonstrations each. LIBERO-10 is left out because CoTracker keeps the whole demonstration in
memory, and LIBERO-10’s 300–460-frame demonstrations need 17–27 GiB, more than our 24 GiB
cards hold. ATM has two stages. The track transformer keeps ATM’s configuration and per-GPU
batch on three GPUs, for a global batch of 1,536 instead of 2,048. The track-guided policy runs out
of memory at the recipe’s batch of 128 and at 64 per GPU, so it trains at 32 per GPU on two GPUs.
Each stage ran under a wall-clock limit set in advance, 36 and 24 h, and we use the checkpoint that
its own validation loss selects. The track transformer reached epoch 40 of 144 and the policy epoch
4 of 5; measured in samples, one policy epoch here equals about 4.5 epochs of the recipe. Before any
number was produced we fixed one bug in the released track dataset: its translation augmentation
shifted the cached tracks in place, so each sample inherited the shifts of earlier ones. We now copy
the slice before augmenting it.
We evaluate two variants. Language is ATM as published. Demonstration tracks keeps the lan-
guage but replaces the predicted tracks at every step with the CoTracker tracks of the episode’s own
demonstration (32 points, taken at the demonstration frame aligned with the current step), so that
ATM reads the demonstration the way our model does. Neither variant completes a held-out episode.
To confirm that the trained checkpoint acts at all, we set a threshold before seeing its numbers: at
least 7 of 70 successes on training tasks. It passes, completing 12 of 70 training-task episodes from
language and 3 of 70 with demonstration tracks, so the held-out failures come from the new tasks
rather than from a broken checkpoint.
H.4
ZERO-WAM
Zero-WAM released two checkpoints of about 11B parameters, one pretrained and one post-trained
on RoboTwin’s bimanual robot, and no single-arm or LIBERO checkpoint. Retraining needs about
173 GB per GPU, so we run a released checkpoint zero-shot. The prompt is the episode’s own
demonstration from the agentview camera at 12 frames per second, with an empty language prompt.
Agentview and eye-in-hand fill its high and left-wrist views, and the right-wrist view is set to zeros.
We execute its left-arm end-effector target and gripper command through the pose servo of the
Instant Policy port; a fixed transform, written before any rollout, rotates RoboTwin’s world frame
by 90◦into LIBERO’s. Three checks were fixed in advance to make sure this pipeline works: the
checkpoint answers a dummy query; replaying each demonstration’s own end-effector trajectory
through the decoding path and the servo succeeds in 9 of 9 episodes; and smoke episodes produce
bounded actions. We chose between the two checkpoints by a rule also set in advance, the median
cosine between the first eight predicted displacements and the demonstration’s next 3 cm of motion;
the post-trained checkpoint won, 0.599 against 0.555. It completes 0 of 30 on each task, with median
25


progress 0. The arm does move, ending a median 23.7, 18.9 and 27.6 cm from its start, but no target
object moves more than 0.06 cm. The setting is far from the checkpoint’s training domain: the
LIBERO simulator, the single Panda and its cameras are all new to it, and the prompt is a robot
video rather than a human one.

META-WORLD
Meta-World defines its own splits. ML45 trains on 45 classes and tests on bin-picking, box-close,
hand-insert, door-lock and door-unlock; ML10 trains on 10 and tests on door-close, drawer-open,
lever-pull, shelf-place and sweep-into. The robot is a Sawyer with a four-dimensional action in
[−1, 1], and the demonstrations come from the benchmark’s scripted experts.
Corpus, entities and checks.
The training corpus takes the 50 tasks listed for each class and
records each one until info[’success’] fires, plus ten further steps (ML45: 2,462 episodes,
211,816 steps, 36 to 50 episodes per training class). The program tracks every body other than the
robot that moves on a free, hinge or slide joint, and describes each with the same nine keypoints.
Because box-close’s scripted expert fails on 6 of its 50 tasks, every class is evaluated on the first
30 tasks its expert solved; this list was fixed from the experts’ record before any model was eval-
uated. Two checks confirm that evaluation reproduces the recorded episodes: resetting from the
stored rand vec reproduces every first frame to within 10−4 m, and replaying the recorded actions
reaches success at the recorded step in all 150 sampled episodes. Meta-World also draws its own
26


target marker in the image. Every variant sees it, including the reference, which receives no task
specification.
Evaluation.
The pointer rule is chosen on validation episodes of the training classes (time pointer:
82 of 91; stage pointer: 69). Each test class is run in two layouts. In the own layout, an episode
is programmed by its own demonstration. In the transferred layout, episode j is programmed by
the demonstration of episode j+1 of the same class; at every re-plan the program is translated
to the object’s current position, with the offset decaying to zero at G⋆. All runs use Kexec=4, a
deterministic seed and 500 steps. The published rows reach the test class differently: they fine-
tune for 200 gradient steps on ten expert episodes of the class, observe a point cloud, and report
the mean of their five best checkpoints over three seeds. Our model changes no weights, reads one
demonstration, and takes entities from simulator state.

RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2024) varies what LIBERO holds fixed. Its 120 procedurally gen-
erated kitchens change layout and style every episode, the robot is a mobile manipulator with a
12-dimensional action, the assets differ, and the benchmark defines 25 atomic tasks. We convert its
1,382 released human demonstrations with the state-replay procedure used for LIBERO. Three tasks
are held out, one per skill family, chosen before any result was computed: PnPCounterToMicrowave,
OpenSingleDoor (whose reverse is in training) and TurnOnStove (a rotation of a fixed object, like
the LIBERO knob).
Adapting the program.
A kitchen contains over a hundred fixtures, so the program keeps only
the movable objects and the joints of the fixtures the task names (RoboCasa’s fixture refs,
28


or the task’s fixture objects where that annotation is missing). RoboCasa places its world frame at
a kitchen corner; following the LIBERO convention, we therefore express programs, entity poses
and end-effector channels in the mobile base’s frame at the episode’s first state. A few keypoints
lie metres away, so position channels are normalised by pooled 1st and 99th percentiles. Each
held-out episode is rebuilt from its demonstration’s scene XML, first state and cameras, and two
checks confirm the rebuild: live keypoints match the program’s first frame to within 10−4 mm, and
replaying the recorded actions through the evaluator reaches RoboCasa’s success check. Textures
were not recorded, so each episode draws its own from the same library, with the same seed across
variants. Step limits are RoboCasa’s horizons (600, 500, 500); everything else follows the LIBERO
protocol.
Recipe and results.
The variant uses the PEWAM recipe, with the contact channel and the paired
corpus, trained on the remaining 22 tasks; each entity’s box is built from its rigid subtree, since
RoboCasa attaches a cabinet door’s panel to child bodies. The reference is the same recipe trained
on the observation alone. The effect program completes 21, 24 and 24 of 30 episodes on the stove,
the door and the microwave, 69 of 90 against 44 for the reference and 50 for the terminal state alone
(Table 20). On the 49 validation episodes of the training tasks, the effect program, the terminal state
alone and the reference complete 32, 27 and 35.

OpenWAM-α.
OpenWAM-α (Wang et al., 2026) pairs a 5B video backbone with an action dif-
fusion transformer and conditions on language, two images and the end-effector state. Its released
LIBERO checkpoint was fine-tuned on all four LIBERO suites, which contain our held-out LIBERO
tasks, so we evaluate it on RoboCasa instead, which appears neither in its fine-tuning data nor in its
listed pretraining data. It runs zero-shot on the same 30 episodes per task, with each episode’s
RoboCasa instruction. The end-effector state is mapped from RoboCasa’s Panda root into LIBERO-
Goal’s pose (states agree to 0.00 mm, and nine held-out demonstrations replay successfully through
this mapping), and the mobile base is held still. As an installation check, it completes 10 of 10
episodes of two LIBERO-Goal tasks. On RoboCasa it completes 2, 5 and 3 of 30 on the stove, the
door and the microwave. This row is therefore not a comparison on shared data: the checkpoint has
never seen RoboCasa, and the mapped end-effector height falls outside its state range on 73% of
demonstration steps.

Real-robot setup.
The robot is a Franka Research 3 with its parallel gripper, controlled at 26 Hz
in operational space. An Intel RealSense D435 RGB-D camera, calibrated to the robot’s base frame
(4 mm reprojection error of the gripper), records both the human demonstrations and the robot’s live
observation. The model is the simulation checkpoint of §4.1 fine-tuned on 156 real-world demon-
strations of other tasks, covering 20 object–container and 10 sequential-manipulation combinations,
none of them one of the four evaluation tasks; the goal-image variant shares its backbone and train-
ing data and is trained with the demonstration’s last frame as its condition in place of the program.
Each task is demonstrated once by a person; the robot never receives a robot demonstration of it. A
trial succeeds when the manipulated object rests inside the designated target region after the gripper
opens; under an edited terminal state, when the cube’s final centre lies within 5 cm of the shifted
target, the tolerance of the reversed-program test in Appendix F; both criteria were fixed before
the first trial. Every condition of Table 4 runs 10 trials from object placements within 8 cm of the
demonstration’s. On cube to plate the five conditions (goal image, the program as demonstrated,
the two shifted terminal states and the push) run in rotation, and the as-demonstrated trials are the
PEWAM entries of the left block; on the other three tasks the two methods alternate. This gives
110 trials in all. Terminal edits shift G⋆by 10 cm to the left and to the right of the demonstrated
placement; the push moves the cube 5 cm by hand at the first re-plan after the gripper closes.

RLBENCH
We evaluate on RLBench (James et al., 2020) because it is the benchmark on which Instant Policy
reports its results, and we hold out all 24 of its tasks. Compared with LIBERO, it changes four things
at once: the simulator is CoppeliaSim rather than MuJoCo, the native action is the next absolute end-
effector pose reached through IK, demonstrations come from scripted planners, and the training set
can only contain tasks for which the planners produce demonstrations.
Training data and filters.
Each candidate training task gets 30 planner attempts within 45 min-
utes, and a task enters training once the planners reach 20 successes (49 tasks did). The program
contains every dynamic or jointed Shape under the task’s base, merged by rigid body and described
by the nine box keypoints in the robot base frame; in scenes with more than 10 entities we keep the
ten nearest the first grasped object. Two checks then remove tasks whose data would be unreliable:
a training task is dropped if replaying it through IK succeeds less than half the time, or if its reset
first frame lies 1 mm or more from the program. This leaves 39 training tasks, 1,136 episodes and
169,960 training windows, none of them from a held-out task.
One recording detail affects two tasks. RLBench derives gripper open from the finger opening,
so in CloseJar and OpenJar, where the fingers close on a thick lid, the recorded channel never leaves
open. For these two tasks the replay reference and Instant Policy therefore take the gripper state
from the demonstration’s grasp record. Our variants read the effect program instead, so they are
unaffected.
Evaluation.
Each held-out episode is reset to the stored state of its own demonstration, and that
demonstration serves as the program. We chose the time pointer on validation episodes of the
30


training tasks (21 of 45 against 13). Episodes use Kexec=4, a deterministic seed, and a budget of
500 steps or twice the demonstration length. When the IK cannot reach a requested pose, that step
is skipped and the model re-plans at the next boundary; the same rule applies to our variants and to
Instant Policy.

Two action heads.
Our first variants predicted the next absolute end-effector pose, which is RL-
Bench’s native action but not the one our model uses on LIBERO. Amendment G therefore retrains
the three variants with the LIBERO action, a 7-D end-effector increment (position scaled by 0.025 m
and rotation by 0.10 rad, gripper last), converted from the same demonstrations. The increment is
added to the commanded setpoint, not to the measured pose: the controller lags the command by
1.5 to 4 mm per step, and integrating the measured pose would accumulate that lag (amendment
G-2). The setpoint is reset to the measured pose at the start of an episode, after an IK rejection, and
whenever the two differ by more than 3 cm or 15◦. As an upper reference for this execution path, re-
playing the held-out demonstrations through it completes 616 of 720, against 636 for absolute-pose
replay.
Results.
Table 21 gives the per-task counts. With the increment head, the effect program completes
160 of the 720 held-out episodes, while the same recipe without a program completes 108. The
margin of 52 exceeds the 48 fixed in advance. The relative program lies in between, at 123. Four
tasks account for most of the margin: PutRubbishInBin (27 against 2), PressSwitch (15 against 2),
ToiletSeatDown (20 against 14) and TakeLidOffSaucepan (17 against 10).
The absolute-pose head orders the variants differently. Here the relative program leads with 134,
followed by the program with 109 and the reference with 93. On the two lid tasks the absolute-pose
program does better than Instant Policy (CloseJar 11 against 9, OpenJar 2 against 0; Figure 22).
Over all tasks, Instant Policy completes 575 with one demonstration and 630 with two.

Execution on RLBench.
Two measurements characterise the episodes: those in which no entity
moves, and requested poses the IK cannot reach. Episodes with no entity moving by a centimetre go
from 398 of 720 with the absolute-pose program to 338 with the increment program, and the share
of executed steps whose requested pose the IK cannot reach is 0.283 for the increment program
against 0.303. Unreachable poses concentrate in a subset of tasks: in the absolute-pose variants a
task with no successes requests 6,800 to 9,400 of them, while LampOn, TakeLidOffSaucepan and
PutRubbishInBin request none. The program’s first frame lies a median of 1 mm or less from the
scene on 22 tasks.
A fallback for rejected poses (amendment H).
Since rejected poses are this common, amend-
ment H changes what the executor does with them. Previously the arm simply held in place. With
the fallback, the executor first retries with the step from the setpoint scaled by 1/2, 1/4 and 1/8,
then keeps the position and holds the orientation at the measured one, and only then holds in place.
Weights and protocol are unchanged, and the three increment variants run the same 720 episodes.
The fallback lowers the share of executed steps that end in a hold from 0.283 to 0.185 for the
program variant and from 0.380 to 0.236 for the reference (Table 22). The reference rises from 108
to 121 successes, gaining on ToiletSeatDown, TakeLidOffSaucepan and CloseMicrowave, whereas
the program rises from 160 to 163, with its gain on PressSwitch (15 to 19). The margin becomes 42;
§4.1 reports the configuration without the fallback.

The forward query.
The forward query asks the model how the object will move under a given
execution. We pose it at every 16-step window of four episodes (the three successes of Figure 3
and the failure of episode 211): the model receives the live scene and the end-effector execution
actually carried out, and predicts the object’s motion (Figure 19). Across 67 windows, predicted and
realised displacement magnitudes correlate at 0.94. The object moves in five windows, by a median
of 8.1 cm, and there the median error is 1.5 cm; in the windows where it stays, the median error is
0.30 cm. In episode 211 the bowl is pushed by at most 1.7 cm and never lifted.
