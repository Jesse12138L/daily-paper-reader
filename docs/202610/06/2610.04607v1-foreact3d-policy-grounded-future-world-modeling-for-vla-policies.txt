# ABSTRACT 

Robots need to anticipate how their actions will change the world, since manip-ulation success hinges on the resulting contacts and object motions. However, existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies that predict future observations from shared features leave the forecast decoupled from the actions the policy will actually execute, and impose no physical constraints on how the scene may evolve. We introduce ForeAct3D, a framework for policy-grounded future world model-ing within VLA policies. Learnable geometric queries decode depth, semantic segmentation, and camera pose from the policy representation into current and future semantic 3D scene states, and the future queries are conditioned on the policy-generated action chunk to ground the forecast in the planned interaction. A physical-consistency closure relates the two states through background static-ity and instance-level rigidity, and anchors the wrist-camera pose to end-effector kinematics. These objectives shape the shared representation used for action gen-eration during training, and no future prediction is required at inference. Without robot pretraining, ForeAct3D achieves 98.3% average success on LIBERO and an average task length of 3.73 on CALVIN, outperforming its base policy on ev-ery suite. Ablations show that semantic 3D supervision, physical consistency, and action conditioning each improve manipulation performance, and that action conditioning substantially improves future object localization. Real-world exper-iments on spatial placement, object insertion, and sequential manipulation further raise average success from 6.7% to 37.8% over the base policy. The project page and code are available at https://github.com/anthonytao80-crypto/ForeAct3D. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Future world modeling aims to predict how a physical scene will evolve under robot actions. This capability matters for manipulation because the same intended action can succeed or fail depending on contact: a push may slide an object toward its target or tip it over, and an insertion may seat or jam. Anticipating such outcomes requires reasoning about both the current observation and the planned action, since the resulting transitions involve contact, changing occlusions, and object motion (Zhen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026a; Huang et al., 2026). Robots therefore need a mechanism that represents the physical world, models its transitions under robot actions, and predicts the resulting future states subject to physical constraints (LeCun et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2026). Existing approaches to future world modeling for robotic manipulation broadly follow two direc-tions. The first models future observations with action-conditioned video generation (Zhu et al., 2025a; Cen et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025b). Such models capture how appearance evolves under actions, but represent the future in image space, leaving the underlying 3D structure and physical constraints implicit. The second extends Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2024) with auxiliary objectives that predict future observations or features from the policy representation (Zhang et al., 2026a; Zheng et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026; Qian et al., 2026). We adopt the VLA setting because future-state supervision shapes the represen-tation used for action generation while retaining direct action inference without test-time rollouts. Achieving physically consistent future world modeling within a VLA policy poses three challenges. First, constructing a task-relevant state requires recovering 3D geometry from policy features and

Second, constraining state transitions requires current and future predictions to jointly satisfy physical constraints: viewpoint changes must be reconciled with a static environment, and moving objects must preserve rigid geometry. Third, grounding the future in action requires predicting the scene changes induced by the robot’s chosen action, since the same scene can evolve differently under different actions. Addressing these challenges calls for a structured state, physical constraints across time, and explicit conditioning on the policy’s planned interaction. To close this gap, we introduce ForeAct3D , an action-conditioned semantic 3D world modeling framework integrated into VLA policies. As illustrated in Figure 1, ForeAct3D constructs current and future scene states from the representation used for action generation. Learnable geometric queries decode depth, three-class semantic masks, and camera pose, jointly capturing scene ge-ometry and its partition into object, robot, and background regions. During training, a physical-consistency closure relates the two states through background staticity, instance-level rigidity, and end-effector kinematics. Future queries additionally receive the policy-generated action chunk, grounding the predicted transition in the planned interaction. Together, these components train the shared VLA representation to encode a semantic 3D scene and its physically constrained evolution over the action horizon, while retaining direct action inference during deployment. We evaluate ForeAct3D on LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) and CALVIN (Mees et al., 2022), achieving 98.3% average success and an average task length of 3.73 without robot pretraining. We further assess the predicted states through geometry, segmentation, and localization metrics, and examine the contributions of structured 3D prediction, physical consistency, and action conditioning through ablations. Real-world experiments on spatial placement, insertion, and sequential manipulation tasks confirm these gains on physical hardware. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce ForeAct3D, a framework for policy-grounded future world modeling within VLA policies that reconstructs current semantic 3D states and predicts future states condi-tioned on policy-generated actions. • We formulate physical-consistency regularization that relates current and future geometry through background staticity and instance rigidity, while grounding wrist-camera pose in end-effector kinematics. • We demonstrate improved policy performance on LIBERO, CALVIN, and real-world tasks, with component ablations assessing policy gains and prediction ablations showing the ben-efit of action conditioning for future object localization.

Predic-tion targets include latent features (Zheng et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026) and visual or geometric observations (Wu et al., 2024a; Zhen et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026a; Qian et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). These objectives encourage anticipation but leave two gaps. First, future prediction alone does not enforce a physically coherent transition from the current scene. Second, shared features alone do not specify which planned action a predicted future corresponds to, motivating explicit action conditioning. ForeAct3D addresses both by jointly predicting current and future semantic 3D states, coupling them through physical-consistency constraints, and conditioning future queries on the policy-generated action chunk. Together, these components supervise structured scene evolution under the planned interaction within the representation used for action generation. 

# 3 METHOD 

3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

Overview. To anticipate the consequences of a planned manipulation, we perform future world modeling through semantic 3D scene prediction within a VLA policy. The model estimates the current scene and predicts its future state over the policy’s action horizon. As illustrated in Figure 2, structured geometry defines the state, physical-consistency constraints relate the two states across time, and explicit action conditioning grounds the predicted future in the policy’s planned action. At time t, the robot receives a language instruction ℓ and an observation set ot = {ovt }v∈V containing images from an external agent camera and a robot-mounted wrist camera, where V denotes these 3two views. The policy π maps (ℓ, o t) to an action chunk at:t+T of horizon T , which also defines the future prediction time t + T . This shared horizon aligns future-state supervision with the scene changes induced by the planned action chunk. We predict the scene state at the action horizon’s endpoint without generating a sequence of observations for the intermediate action steps. 

Semantic 3D scene state. To capture the geometry relevant to manipulation, we represent each scene state using depth, three-class semantic masks, and camera pose. Depth encodes spatial struc-ture, semantic masks separate background, robot, and object regions, and camera pose places each view in a common world frame. All objects share a single object class; the prediction neither assigns instance identities nor establishes object correspondence across the current and future states. For each view v, depth Dvt , known intrinsics Kv , and camera pose Evt define a point cloud Pvt by back-projection Π−1. The semantic mask Svt labels these points, so the scene state combines metric geometry with the semantic role of each region: 

Pvt = Π −1(Dvt , K v , E vt ) , Gt = {Dvt , S vt , E vt }v∈V . (1) ForeAct3D estimates the current state from the instruction and observations, and additionally con-ditions the future estimate on the planned action chunk. Both estimates live in the same semantic 3D representation, which allows physical constraints to relate the observed scene to its configuration after the action chunk: 

ˆGt = fθ (ot, ℓ ), ˆGt+T = fθ (ot, ℓ, a t:t+T ). (2) The current estimate recovers the scene the policy observes, whereas the future estimate predicts the consequences of the action it generates. This formulation supervises both endpoints of the interaction in a single representation and makes their geometric relationship part of policy learning. 3.2 SEMANTIC GEOMETRY FROM POLICY FEATURES 

Geometric queries. To extract a structured scene state from the policy representation, we introduce six learnable queries q(m,τ ), indexed by geometric quantity m ∈ { depth , seg , pose } and prediction time τ ∈ { t, t + T }. Each query cross-attends to the final backbone features Ht to produce a query feature z(m,τ ); future queries are further conditioned on the action chunk as described in Section 3.4. View embeddings are added before decoding: depth and segmentation queries serve both cameras, while pose queries use the wrist-view embedding to predict only the wrist-camera pose. Depth and segmentation share a decoder that attends to intermediate-layer backbone features of the corresponding view, followed by separate output heads. Because all queries read from the policy backbone, geometric supervision directly shapes the representation used for control. Ground-truth instance IDs provide supervision distinct from the three predicted semantic classes. The rigidity constraint in Section 3.3 uses these IDs to partition the predicted geometry into in-dividual objects and applies Kabsch alignment to each instance using pairs at shared valid pixels. This supplies instance-level physical supervision without requiring the segmentation head to predict object identities or establish object correspondence between the current and future scene states. 3.3 PHYSICAL -C ONSISTENCY CLOSURE 

We impose three complementary constraints on predicted geometry and camera pose: background staticity, instance-level rigidity, and end-effector kinematics. The two geometric constraints operate on agent-view point clouds back-projected from predicted depth with ground-truth intrinsics and extrinsics. Since the agent camera is fixed, its extrinsics are identical at both endpoints, and the resulting points are directly comparable in world coordinates. Labeled calibration isolates depth consistency from pose prediction errors, while kinematics supervises the wrist-camera pose. 

Background staticity. To keep the static environment fixed over time, we compare current and future background points back-projected from predicted depth at the same image pixels. A shared mask retains pixels labeled as valid background at both endpoints, thereby excluding regions oc-cluded by the robot or objects at either time. The background loss Lbg averages a Smooth-L1 distance d(·, ·) over these paired points; the same distance is used in the rigidity term below. 

Instance-level rigidity. To preserve object shape under motion, we require each instance’s current and future geometry to be related by a rigid transform. End-effector kinematics. To anchor the predicted wrist-camera pose to the robot configuration, we supervise both endpoints using a reference pose from forward kinematics and the fixed hand– eye transform. The pose loss Lee sums equally weighted translation MSE and geodesic rotation distance in radians over both prediction times. We convert the predicted 6D rotation into a rotation matrix before computing the loss. Pose supervision is separate from the background and rigidity constraints, which reconstruct world points using predicted depth and labeled camera poses.

We report manipulation results on LIBERO and CALVIN (Section 4.1) and assess predicted ge-ometry, semantics, and future object localization (Section 4.2). We ablate individual components (Section 4.4) and evaluate three real-world tabletop manipulation tasks (Section 4.3). 4.1 MANIPULATION PERFORMANCE 

LIBERO. Table 1 reports success rates across four suites under two training protocols: one model per suite or one model for all suites. Using a single model for all suites, ForeAct3D achieves 98.3% average success, the highest among listed methods under this protocol. It outperforms its base policy StarVLA-OFT by 1.7 percentage points, with gains across all four suites and the largest on Goal and Long, supporting the benefit of structured 3D prediction without suite-specific adaptation. 

CALVIN. We evaluate long-horizon language-conditioned manipulation in the ABC →D setting, focusing on methods without robot pretraining. # 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented ForeAct3D, a framework for future world modeling in semantic 3D space that com-bines structured scene prediction, physical-consistency closure, and action-conditioned forecasting within VLA policies. ForeAct3D achieves 98.3% average success on LIBERO and an average task length of 3.73 on CALVIN, with ablations supporting complementary contributions from its three components. Real-world cumulative ablations increase average success from 6.7% to 37.8% across three tabletop tasks, with action conditioning improving target-region retention in the evaluated trials. These results support physically constrained future-state prediction as a useful form of su-pervision for robot policy learning. LIBERO. We evaluate on four LIBERO task suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long. Each suite contains 10 tasks, with 50 evaluation episodes per task and 500 episodes per suite. A single policy is trained across all four suites. We report per-suite success rates and their equally weighted average, covering spatial reasoning, object manipulation, goal-conditioned behavior, and long-horizon tasks. 

CALVIN. We adopt the ABC →D setting, training on environments A, B, and C and evaluating zero-shot on the unseen environment D. Evaluation consists of 1,000 rollouts, each containing a sequence of five language-conditioned subtasks. Performance is measured by the average number of successfully completed consecutive subtasks per rollout, with a maximum value of 5. Table 2 additionally reports success rates for completing successive subtasks in the sequence. 

Implementation details. We use the same training configuration for LIBERO and CALVIN, in-cluding all ablation variants. Models are trained using AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) with 

β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .95 , optimizer ϵ = 10 −8, and weight decay 10 −8. The peak learning rate is 

2.5 × 10 −5 for the vision-language backbone and 1.0 × 10 −4 for both the action module and ge-ometric prediction heads. We apply linear warmup followed by cosine decay, with the scheduler’s minimum learning-rate parameter set to 2 × 10 −7, and clip the global gradient norm to 1.0. Training runs for 30,000 optimization steps, including 5,000 learning-rate warmup steps, on 16 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 40 GB of memory each. The batch size is 8 per GPU with no gradi-ent accumulation, giving a global batch size of 128. Ablation variants retain these settings while omitting the specified modules and their associated losses; absent prediction heads receive no super-vision. Physical constraints and dense supervision are defined in Appendices A.1 and A.2. The current- and future-head weights and the overall scene-supervision multiplier are all 1.0. The three closure terms have unit relative weights. Their overall coefficient increases linearly from 0 to 0.1 over the first 2,000 training iterations and remains at 0.1 thereafter; this schedule is separate from the learning-rate warmup for the backbone and prediction heads. A.7 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS 

ForeAct3D assumes static backgrounds and rigid instances, limiting applicability to deformable objects, articulated instances, and moving backgrounds. Same-pixel instance membership approx-imates temporal correspondence without tracking material points and becomes less reliable under large motion. Training requires dense geometric supervision and instance annotations, making pre-dictions sensitive to depth and mask errors. Broader task and robot coverage are needed to as-sess generalization and variability. Geometric comparisons use different prediction horizons, and predicted-mask localization retains ground-truth instance IDs. Future prediction provides training supervision but is unused at inference. Future work could reduce supervision, extend physical con-straints to general scenes, and use predicted futures to evaluate or refine actions. 18
