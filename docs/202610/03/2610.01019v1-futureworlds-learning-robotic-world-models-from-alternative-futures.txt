ABSTRACT
Robotic world models predict action-conditioned future scenes, providing a foun-
dation for understanding action outcomes.
However, turning alternative pre-
dictions into useful learning signals remains challenging: similar candidates
limit informative quality comparisons, while diverging trajectories require per-
sistent maintenance of their individual histories. We introduce FutureWorlds,
a framework that unifies candidate construction, history maintenance, and learn-
ing from relative quality. Built on a multimodal discrete autoregressive model,
FutureWorlds uses diverse beam search during reinforcement learning to con-
struct candidate futures that balance confidence and diversity. Candidate-specific
bounded memory preserves scene states and ensures that generation and pol-
icy scoring use matching histories.
We further propose MemSPO (Memory-
Conditioned Search-Guided Policy Optimization), which converts video trajec-
tory rewards into group-relative advantages to optimize the world model. On RT-
1, BridgeV2, and RoboCasa, FutureWorlds reduces LPIPS for 32-frame pre-
dictions by 14.78%, 20.84%, and 9.12%, respectively, relative to the strongest
baseline on each dataset. Under fixed evaluation configurations, only 200 MemSPO
updates further improve generation quality and support continued prediction be-
yond the training horizon. Memory ablations, decoding sensitivity analysis, and
optical-flow evaluation show that these gains extend beyond visual quality to more
accurate motion prediction and more consistent object states. Project page and
code: https://github.com/Alexander-wu/FutureWorlds.

INTRODUCTION
Robotic world models predict how scenes evolve in response to actions, providing a foundation
for action-outcome prediction (Finn et al., 2016), visual planning (Ebert et al., 2018; Hafner et al.,
2019), behavior learning (Hafner et al., 2020), and policy evaluation (Yin et al., 2026; GigaWorld
Team et al., 2026). Given the same observations, action sequence, and instruction, a model can
propose multiple predictions of the future. These alternative futures represent different hypotheses
about the scene’s evolution, with varying robot motions, object states, and prediction quality. Their
differences offer a learning opportunity: by constructing useful alternatives and comparing their
outcomes against recorded observations, a world model can learn from the predictions it generates.
Most autoregressive world models learn from recorded trajectories through supervised training.
Their reliance on ground-truth histories during training differs from the self-generated histories
available at inference, allowing errors to accumulate along a rollout (Ross et al., 2011; Bengio
et al., 2015). Token-level likelihood also does not directly optimize complete generated sequences
(Ranzato et al., 2016; Rennie et al., 2017). Figure 1 illustrates this challenge: supervised fine-tuning
(SFT) can leave predictions with insufficient robot motion or inaccurate poses. Reinforcement learn-
ing (RL) connects generated outputs to sequence-level rewards (Black et al., 2024). In world mod-
eling, RLVR-World uses decoded prediction metrics (Wu et al., 2025a), while RLIR introduces
inverse-dynamics rewards for action following (Ye et al., 2025). These advances enable learning
from trajectory feedback, but the resulting learning signal also depends on which futures the model
compares. More candidates alone do not guarantee useful comparative feedback. Effective post-
training therefore requires informative candidate differences and history-consistent comparisons that
turn alternative futures into reliable learning signals.

How can we construct informative alternative futures, maintain their individual
histories, and learn from their relative quality to improve robotic world models?
We introduce FutureWorlds, a framework that learns robotic world models by constructing, main-
taining, and comparing alternative futures (Figure 2). We instantiate the framework with a mul-
timodal discrete autoregressive model (van den Oord et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2024), whose token
probabilities support candidate expansion and trajectory scoring under visual, action, and language
conditioning. To construct alternatives, diverse beam search combines model likelihood with cross-
candidate differences during RL training. To maintain their histories, candidate-specific memory
retains scene and state anchors alongside rolling context, supporting longer predictions within a
bounded memory budget. Search and memory operate together as each candidate evolves, and
generation and policy scoring use matching histories. To compare and learn, MemSPO (Memory-
Conditioned Search-Guided Policy Optimization) converts video trajectory rewards into group-
relative advantages and updates the model through a search-guided surrogate based on GRPO (Shao
et al., 2024) and clipped policy optimization (Schulman et al., 2017). Candidate quality differences
thus become feedback for improving the model that generates them.
Our evaluation covers RT-1 (Brohan et al., 2023), BridgeData V2 (Walke et al., 2023), and Robo-
Casa (Nasiriany et al., 2026). On 32-frame predictions, FutureWorlds reduces LPIPS by 14.78%,
20.84%, and 9.12% relative to the strongest baseline on each dataset. Under fixed evaluation config-
urations, only 200 MemSPO updates improve generation and support prediction beyond the training
horizon. Memory ablations, decoding sensitivity analysis, and motion evaluation further assess his-
torical context, robustness, and dynamic consistency. Our contributions are summarized as follows:
❶Learning from Alternative Futures. We introduce FutureWorlds, a framework that organizes
model-generated futures into training experiences through coordinated candidate construction,
history maintenance, and relative comparison.
❷Search-Guided, History-Consistent Learning.
We propose MemSPO, a GRPO-based post-
training algorithm that combines diverse candidate search with candidate-specific memory and
matches the histories used for generation and policy scoring.

RELATED WORK
Robotic World Models. Robotic world models predict action consequences for planning and pol-
icy learning. Early work uses action-conditioned video prediction for visual control (Finn et al.,
2016; Ebert et al., 2018); PlaNet (Hafner et al., 2019) and Dreamer (Hafner et al., 2020) learn la-
tent dynamics. UniSim (Yang et al., 2024) combines language and low-level controls for visual
simulation, while Vid2World (Huang et al., 2026) adapts video diffusion for interactive prediction.
VideoGPT (Yan et al., 2021) and iVideoGPT (Wu et al., 2024) develop discrete autoregressive mod-
els, with iVideoGPT integrating observations, actions, and rewards through compressive tokeniza-
tion. FutureWorlds uses discrete autoregression to construct and compare alternative futures under
shared visual, action, and language conditions.
Memory for Long-Horizon World Modeling.
Transformer-XL (Dai et al., 2019) propagates
segment-level history, while StreamingLLM (Xiao et al., 2024) retains initial attention sinks and
recent context. StreamingT2V (Henschel et al., 2025) combines short- and long-term memory;
FramePack (Zhang & Agrawala, 2025) packs historical frames into a fixed context budget. Wu et al.
(2025b) introduce geometry-grounded memory for scene revisits. Ctrl-World (Guo et al., 2026)
and Mem-World (Zheng et al., 2026) retrieve robotic observations through pose conditioning and
spatiotemporal surfel indexing, respectively. FutureWorlds maintains bounded, candidate-specific
histories for long-horizon prediction and uses matching contexts for generation and policy scoring,
preserving distinct conditioning as candidate futures diverge.
Search-Guided Reinforcement Learning for World Models. MIXER (Ranzato et al., 2016) and
SCST (Rennie et al., 2017) optimize sequence metrics through policy gradients; DDPO (Black et al.,
2024) extends reward optimization to diffusion models. RLVR-World (Wu et al., 2025a) rewards de-
coded prediction quality, while RLIR (Ye et al., 2025) evaluates action following through inverse
dynamics. Candidate construction determines which predictions provide this feedback. Diverse
Beam Search (Vijayakumar et al., 2018) reduces redundancy, Expert Iteration (Anthony et al., 2017)
alternates search and policy learning, and BGRPO (Lee et al., 2025) combines beam-generated
candidates with rank-aware rewards for algebraic tasks. QWM (Dong et al., 2026) searches pre-
dicted futures for robotic action selection and learns policies and values from real transitions.
FutureWorlds instead searches futures under a fixed action sequence. MemSPO updates the video
predictor from their relative quality using history-consistent scoring and a search-guided surrogate
based on GRPO’s group-relative advantages and clipped updates (Shao et al., 2024).

METHOD
Figure 2 presents FutureWorlds, which learns from alternative futures through coordinated candi-
date construction, history maintenance, and relative comparison. A multimodal autoregressive world
model first learns conditional video prediction through supervised training. During post-training, di-
verse search constructs candidate futures under shared visual, action, and language conditions. Each
candidate retains its own history, with bounded persistent memory supporting continuation beyond
the pretraining horizon. MemSPO compares decoded trajectories through video rewards and con-
verts their relative quality into world-model updates. Generation and policy scoring use matching
candidate histories, while true future frames enter only reward computation during post-training.

EXPERIMENTS
Our experiments address four research questions: ❶RQ1 How well does FutureWorlds predict
future observations? ❷RQ2 Can MemSPO improve generation with few post-training updates?
❸RQ3 Do generation gains translate into more accurate motion and dynamics? ❹RQ4 Does mem-
ory maintenance improve prediction beyond the training horizon?
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Baselines. We compare four baseline categories: ✦video generation models: FitVid (Babaeizadeh
et al., 2021) and iVideoGPT (Wu et al., 2024); ✦action-conditioned world models: IRASim (Zhu
et al., 2025), Vid2World (Huang et al., 2026), Ctrl-World (Guo et al., 2026), DriftWorld (Lu et al.,
2026), and WEAVER (Jain et al., 2026); ✦RL post-training methods: RLVR-World (Wu et al.,
2025a) and PersistWorld (Bardhan et al., 2026); and ✦adapted video foundation models: Wan2.2-
TI2V-5B (Team Wan et al., 2025), Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B (NVIDIA, 2025), and DreamDojo-
2B (Gao et al., 2026). Adaptation and evaluation details appear in Appendix B.4.
Datasets and Evaluation. We evaluate action-conditioned video generation on RT-1 (Brohan et al.,
2023), BridgeV2 (Walke et al., 2023), and RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2026), covering real and
simulated manipulation. Table 1 compares 32-frame predictions on 128 fixed held-out trajectories
per dataset using PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018). Methods share trajectories and
target frames, with predictions resized to the reference resolution. Metric definitions and evaluation
details appear in Appendix B.4.
Implementation Details. We train FutureWorlds in PyTorch on 16 NVIDIA H20 GPUs across
two servers. Starting from SFT, MemSPO uses AdamW with learning rate 10−6 and freezes the visual
tokenizer and text encoder. Diverse beam search uses four groups of two beams, retaining one
trajectory per group for reward comparison and updates. Main evaluations use a beam width of four
and persistent memory. Pretrained initialization, SFT, and RL settings appear in Appendix B.1.
4.2
VIDEO GENERATION QUALITY (RQ1)
In Table 1, FutureWorlds leads all three metrics on all datasets. Relative to the strongest baseline
per metric on BridgeV2, it gains 1.71 dB PSNR and 2.17% SSIM and reduces LPIPS by 20.84%.
LPIPS also decreases by 14.78% on RT-1 and 9.12% on RoboCasa relative to the strongest baseline
per dataset. Figure 3 shows closer object locations and arm poses in RT-1, fewer object substitutions
and local distortions in BridgeV2, and more accurate arm and gripper poses in RoboCasa.

MOTION AND DYNAMIC PREDICTION (RQ3)
Using WorldArena’s flow and feature components (Shang et al., 2026), we evaluate 128 trajectories

reducing flow error by 12.40%, 4.57%, and 16.68% versus SFT on RT-1, BridgeV2, and RoboCasa.
Improvements in both flow error and magnitude gap support closer motion agreement rather than
merely larger movements. The shared-scale examples (b) complement these cohort-level results.
4.5
MEMORY MAINTENANCE (RQ4)
We fix weights and decoding and vary only the retained history: the latest state, six recent states,
or an initial anchor plus six recent states. Full memory leads PSNR, SSIM, and MSE at 32 frames
across all datasets (Figure 7), reducing MSE by 40.83%, 57.69%, and 29.22% versus removing
historical memory on RT-1, BridgeV2, and RoboCasa. Recent history supplies most gains; the
anchor adds smaller benefits. As no variant is retrained, this comparison isolates the contribution of
retained context at inference. Appendix F reports settings and results across horizons.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
We present FutureWorlds, which learns from alternative futures through coordinated candidate
construction, history maintenance, and quality comparison. MemSPO combines diverse search with
history-consistent optimization, improving generation within hundreds of updates and beyond the
training horizon. Three-dataset evaluations support gains in visual quality, object consistency, and
motion prediction. BridgeV2 comparisons also show better quality with fewer parameters and lower
measured memory usage, although autoregressive beam search incurs latency. Future work focuses
on faster decoding and validating these benefits in closed-loop planning and robot control.

Scope. These statements concern the reward component at 𝜃old; the reference-regularization gradient
can remain nonzero under collapse, and clipping can become active away from this point. Because
search changes the candidate distribution, the analysis implies neither an unbiased on-policy gradient
nor monotonic improvement. The benefit of diverse beam search remains an empirical question. In
our implementation, each fresh candidate set receives one update evaluated at the old parameters,
where clipping is locally inactive. If 𝜎𝑅≤10−6, the implementation sets all advantages to zero;
the reward gradient is then zero and the magnitude bound remains valid, although the unguarded
pairwise equality need not hold for small nonzero reward differences. The full optimizer step also
includes reference regularization and optimizer preconditioning, so it is distinct from g𝑅.

IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
B.1
PRETRAINED INITIALIZATION, SFT, AND RL
Pretrained initialization and tokenizer adaptation. We initialize from released pretrained visual-
tokenizer and autoregressive world-model checkpoints. RT-1 reuses the pretrained tokenizer di-
rectly; BridgeV2 and RoboCasa use dataset-adapted tokenizers.
Adaptation optimizes the re-
construction objective in Eq. 3, with unit weights on L1 and VGG-LPIPS and an adversarial
weight of 0.1. We use a global batch size of 32, a learning rate of 5 × 10−5, and 5,000 warmup
steps. The BridgeV2 and RoboCasa tokenizer checkpoints use 60,000 and 50,000 shared gener-
ator/discriminator steps, respectively; these counters do not denote generator-only updates. The
resulting tokenizers remain frozen during world-model SFT and RL.
Supervised fine-tuning. Each dataset receives 50,000 SFT updates with global batch size 64.
Teacher forcing supplies recorded histories, and cross-entropy supervises target dynamic tokens
only. AdamW uses a backbone learning rate of 10−5, a text-adapter learning rate of 10−4 where
applicable, zero weight decay, and 500 warmup updates. RT-1 and RoboCasa use cosine decay to
one tenth of the peak learning rate; BridgeV2 keeps the learning rate constant after warmup. Gra-
dient norms are clipped at 1.0. We update the autoregressive backbone, action embeddings, and
text adapters while freezing the visual tokenizer and T5 encoder. RT-1 and RoboCasa use language
conditioning; BridgeV2 omits it. The SFT checkpoint initializes both the trainable RL policy and
its frozen reference.
Reinforcement-learning post-training. MemSPO uses ten-frame predicted rollouts, global batch
size 16, and fresh AdamW state with learning rate 10−6 and zero weight decay. Four search groups
contain two beams each, with diversity penalty 0.1; one trajectory per group enters reward com-
parison. The clipping threshold is 0.2, the reference-regularization coefficient is 0.001, and the
gradient-norm limit is 1.0. Each fresh candidate set receives one optimizer update. Candidate gen-
eration and policy scoring retain the same scene/state anchors and six recent states; future ground-
truth frames enter reward computation only. Main-table checkpoints use 500/200/400 updates for
RT-1/BridgeV2/RoboCasa, whereas the matched GRPO–MemSPO comparison uses 200 updates on
every dataset. Dataset-specific reward choices are identified in Appendix B.3; Algorithm 1 details
the update.

CANDIDATE DIVERSITY AND RELATIVE LEARNING SIGNALS
We inspect saved candidates from the first BridgeV2 training batch, before either strategy updates the
shared SFT checkpoint. Both strategies use the same inputs, frozen tokenizer, ten-frame horizon,
and reward. Ordinary beam search maintains eight global paths and retains the top four; diverse
search uses four groups of two with a diversity penalty of 0.1, retaining one candidate per group.
Figure 15: Candidate trajectories and relative learning signals. Ordinary and diverse beam search
from the same SFT model and input. All four retained candidates appear in their original order at
frames +2, +6, and +10. Annotations give the original ten-frame reward and group-relative advan-
tage 𝐴. The illustrated input has the largest diverse-search reward range among the 16 inputs in the
initial batch.
Ordinary search produces nearly identical visible continuations, whereas diverse search exposes
differences in gripper configuration and object appearance. Positive and negative advantages give
the RL objective alternatives to promote or suppress. Across all 16 inputs in this initial batch, mean
pairwise token disagreement is 0.28% for ordinary search and 30.32% for diverse search; the mean
within-group reward range is 6.44×10−5 and 8.66×10−3, respectively. Token disagreement averages
over all six candidate pairs and 10 × 80 token positions; it is distinct from perceptual diversity.
Diverse search also includes weaker candidates: its mean reward is −0.14353, compared with
−0.14041 for ordinary search. These diagnostics illustrate reduced candidate redundancy and a
broader quality distribution, rather than an immediate increase in average quality. Since advan-
tages are group-normalized, a wider raw reward range alone does not establish stronger gradients or
improved post-training performance. Table 5 complements these candidate-level diagnostics with
final-model results.

Results and interpretation. Table 6 reports the three metrics shown in Figure 7 at all evaluated
horizons. Retaining recent history yields the largest improvement over removing historical memory
on every dataset. At 32 frames, the full model achieves the best PSNR, SSIM, and MSE, reducing
MSE by 40.83%, 57.69%, and 29.22% relative to the history-removed variant on RT-1, BridgeV2,
and RoboCasa. The initial anchor provides smaller additional gains. Its effect varies with horizon
and metric: on RoboCasa, removing the anchor yields slightly lower MSE at 10 and 20 frames,
while the full model retains higher PSNR and SSIM. These results distinguish the substantial role
of recent context from the more modest contribution of a persistent anchor. We report cohort means
without inferring statistical significance.

Parameter accounting.
Inference parameters include the prediction backbone, action/history
adapters, and visual encoder–decoder, counting tied tensors once. Offline language encoders and un-
used task, reward, or critic modules are excluded. Loaded parameters additionally include retained
unused modules inventoried in the native pipeline: WEAVER retains a task encoder and reward/critic
networks, while PersistWorld retains unused image and text encoders.
FutureWorlds contains
127,101,696 backbone parameters and 129,448,016 visual-codec parameters, totaling 256,549,712.
Its frozen RL reference and training reward network are absent from inference.
Warmup and timing. We preload input image/action arrays and model weights, disable gradients
and TF32, and perform one complete 32-frame warmup. We then time each of the three cases once
using a wall-clock timer, synchronizing CUDA immediately before and after inference. The table
reports the median and observed range, not a confidence interval. Timing includes input preprocess-
ing, CPU–GPU transfers, visual encoding, prediction, decoding, resizing, and transfer of the output
to CPU. Model loading, trajectory-file reading, video saving, and quality metrics are excluded. Text
features are precomputed. Wan loads and transfers its cached instruction embedding inside the timed
call; this cache-file access is included, while language encoding is excluded.
Memory and residency. Before each measured call, we reset PyTorch’s CUDA peak-memory coun-
ters after synchronization. Allocated memory counts live tensor allocations; reserved memory also
includes the allocator’s cached blocks. We report the maximum of each over the three calls, in
GiB (230 bytes). Figure 5 uses allocated memory. These measurements include resident model ten-
sors and inference intermediates, not just incremental activation memory. Active components reside
on GPU; the benchmark does not add dynamic CPU offloading. WEAVER’s unused EMA copy
moves to CPU before warmup, whereas the unused modules counted above remain in the native
GPU pipeline. The resulting memory costs describe these implementations, rather than minimum
architectural requirements.
